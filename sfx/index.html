13Fire, air and confetti

The short version: The effects that mark the drop - CO2 jets, confetti, flames, sparks, pyro and kabuki drops - are all governed by fire codes and local permits, and in the US by two NFPA standards in particular. Most fatal incidents follow one pattern: sparks indoors, near a flammable ceiling or acoustic foam. And "cold spark" machines are not cold; at least some fire departments, and NFPA 1126's definitions, treat them as pyrotechnics.

CO2 jets and cryo

A CO2 jet does one thing: it opens a valve, fires a column of liquid carbon dioxide, and closes again. The white plume is "frozen humidity", gone within seconds, leaving nothing on the floor326. The usual setup is one siphon tank, one hose and one jet; valves are rated to at least 1,450 psi, and plumes reach about 8-20 feet on small jets, 35-55 feet on DMX valve jets and 75-90 feet on multi-valve units, shorter in heat and dry air326. One supplier lists 15-20 feet of audience clearance, notes that every type of CO2 effect needs a permit in Las Vegas, and recommends giving effects their own DMX universe so noise on a shared line cannot cause a misfire326.

The hazard is the gas, not the cold. CO2 is about 1.5 times as dense as air and collects in low spots. NIOSH and OSHA set a shift-average limit of 5,000 ppm, and NIOSH lists 40,000 ppm as immediately dangerous to life or health; symptoms run from headache and dizziness to convulsions and asphyxia, plus frostbite from the liquid327. Industrial gas guidance puts alarm thresholds at below 19.5% oxygen or above 0.5% CO2, and stresses that CO2 is a health hazard in itself, not only an oxygen displacer328. The body's urge to breathe is driven by CO2 rather than by low oxygen, so a person in an oxygen-poor pit can pass out with little warning, and readings can be fine at chest height and unsafe at floor level329. The highest-risk scenario, in our reading, is CO2 released into a low, enclosed, poorly ventilated space: an orchestra pit, a sunken stage, a basement club.

Confetti and streamers

Electric confetti cannons fire on a button or trigger, with ranges of up to 12 meters (about 40 feet); one major maker's tissue confetti is listed as certified biodegradable (in named colors) and flameproof to the German B1 standard330. Not every "biodegradable" claim carries a certificate, and water-soluble confetti only dissolves when soaked and agitated; if it dries first, it can stick to floors and become a slip hazard331.

Flame effects

A stage flame unit's safety logic is layered. On one widely used design, one DMX channel arms it and a second fires it; it arms only when the safety channel sits between 40% and 60%, then counts down for three seconds while it self-checks, and any other value drops it back to safe mode332. A tilt sensor shuts it down if it is knocked, there is a latching emergency stop, and the operator must be able to see every unit; the manufacturer specifies a 3-meter danger radius with 6 to 10 meters clear overhead, and the flames reach 3 to 6 meters332. Practitioners add their own redundancy: flame units on their own universe, a manned emergency stop on the power, and a dead-man handle that enables the effect while DMX only handles the timing - because DMX is one-way and has no error checking333.

Pyro, and the standards that govern it

In the US, NFPA 1126 covers pyrotechnics before a proximate audience - musical, theatrical and similar productions, indoors and out - and, according to a published summary of its 2026 edition, now requires every operator to be a "pyrotechnic professional"334. NFPA 160 covers flame effects before an audience, from design to defueling; the same kind of summary says its 2026 edition makes the operator, not production management, responsible for deciding how long safe removal takes335. States and cities adopt NFPA 1126 into their own law336, and local fire departments issue specific permits for proximate pyro337. Federal rules define categories such as display fireworks and "theatrical flash powder" in kits of an ounce or less338. State licenses vary; North Carolina's 1.4G operator license, for example, requires the holder to be 21 or older, complete 8 hours of training, have lead-operator experience on three shows and pass an exam339.

"Cold spark" machines are not cold

The machines sold as "cold spark" feed a powder of roughly 95% titanium and 5% zirconium into a tube heated to around 560 C until it glows, then blow it out through a nozzle; the patent claims the falling sparks cool to a touch-safe temperature340. Fire officials disagree with the framing. Mesa, Arizona's fire department says "there is no such thing as 'cold sparks' or pyrotechnic effects that are safer than others", notes that titanium powder can self-ignite in air as low as 482 F, and says NFPA 1126's definitions include cold spark technology341. West Milford, New Jersey, requires a permit, operator certification, insurance, 10 feet of clearance from combustibles, a Class D metal-fire extinguisher and possibly a paid fire watch342. A more accurate description, in our view, is "a lower-temperature spark effect, still regulated as pyrotechnics".

Kabuki drops

A kabuki drop releases a curtain or banner on cue so it falls away to reveal what is behind it. Electromagnetic release units can hold loads up to about 50 kg and are triggered remotely343.

Who fires the effects, and how

Pyro and flame are run by licensed or certified operators under a local permit334339. Effects are usually cued over DMX on a dedicated universe, with a separate arm or safety channel and a manual enable held by a person who can see the device332326333. Some crews put a second operator with a better view on the safety while the main desk handles timing333. For DJ shows, where the track structure is not fixed, the drop is usually fired by hand, or armed in advance and released by hand; full timecode suits pre-built show files. That last point is our inference.

The fires that wrote the rules

On 20 February 2003, at The Station nightclub in West Warwick, Rhode Island, pyrotechnic gerbs set off by a touring band's manager ignited polyurethane acoustic foam around the stage. One hundred people died and 230 were injured; the club had no sprinklers and was over its licensed capacity of 404344. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology found conditions at head height in the middle of the room became lethal "in less than 90 seconds after ignition of foam", and recommended sprinklers in all new nightclubs and existing ones over 100 occupants, a ban on indoor pyro in nightclubs without sprinklers, a ban on non-fire-retarded foam as a finish, and designing on the assumption that the main exit is blocked345. The NFPA amended its Life Safety Code within months to require sprinklers in existing nightclubs over 100 occupants and crowd managers344.

The pattern repeats:

  • Club Pulse, Kočani, North Macedonia, 16 March 2025 - 59 dead in the first reports, rising to 63 by October 2025 as the injured died. Sparks from stage pyrotechnics ignited acoustic foam on the ceiling, and investigators later found the club's operating license was forged346347.
  • Le Constellation, Crans-Montana, Switzerland, New Year's 2026 - 40 dead in the first counts and 41 by February 2026, with about 115 to 119 injured; the preliminary cause is sparklers on champagne bottles held too close to the ceiling, with acoustic foam under examination348349.
  • Formosa Fun Coast, Taiwan, 27 June 2015 - 15 dead and about 500 burned when colored cornstarch powder sprayed over a crowd at a party with DJs ignited. Not pyro, but an effect aimed at an audience350.
  • Tomorrowland, July 2025 - the Orbyz mainstage caught fire during a final pyrotechnic rehearsal two days before the festival, and no one was hurt99. A Belgian newspaper later reported, citing sources close to the investigation, that an accidental ethanol spill during subcontractors' testing of the stage's fire bowls may have caused it; that is unconfirmed, and Tomorrowland has not addressed the cause351.

The lesson (ours, from the pattern): the device is only half of it. Finishes, sprinklers and exits decide whether a spark becomes a disaster.

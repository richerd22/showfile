18Who does what

The short version: The tour manager owns the money, the schedule and the people. Production, sound, lighting, video, lasers, rigging and effects crews sit under that umbrella, joined on show day by the venue's own staff. Some of those jobs - riggers, laser operators, pyro operators - need certificates or licenses; most are learned on the job.

The roles with solid public definitions:

  • Tour manager. Usually hired freelance per tour by the agent or artist manager. Builds the tour budget (crew pay, per diems, lodging, transport, equipment, visas, rehearsals, commissions), advances each show, settles the fee with the promoter after the show, pays per diems, prepares the next day's schedule and guest list, and handles passes435. A road manager handles one act's details; one person can do both jobs435.
  • Front-of-house and monitor engineers. FOH runs the audience mix; monitors runs what performers hear436. At a DJ show, as noted above, much of the FOH job is managing level and protecting the system.
  • System engineer. Arrives first, measures the room, assembles and tunes the PA, and walks the coverage during the show309.
  • Lighting designer. Creates the look within the limits of visibility, safety and cost, and on small tours also programs and runs the desk221.
  • Riggers. Hang the chain hoists that lift truss, LED walls and scenery from the building's structure. The head rigger often marks points on the floor and works out bridle lengths by trigonometry437. Certified in the US through ETCP (Arena and Theatre Rigger) and in the UK through the PLASA National Rigging Certificate437438.
  • Laser operator. Must be "a trained, competent operator" in direct personal control who can see every beam path (or has a spotter), with emergency stops always within reach223.
  • Pyro and flame operators. Licensed or certified under state and local rules; NFPA 1126's 2026 edition, per a published summary, requires "pyrotechnic professionals"334.

The rest are standard practitioner definitions that public sources do not formally pin down, so treat them as trade usage:

  • Promoter - takes the financial risk on a show, rents or partners with the venue, pays the artist, and splits what is left.
  • Agent - represents the artist and sells dates. The talent buyer works for the venue or promoter.
  • Production manager - owns the technical advance, vendors, crew calls and production schedule.
  • Stage manager - runs the stage and the changeovers on show day.
  • Programmer and operator - build the lighting show file, then run it nightly.
  • Backline tech - at an electronic dance music show, looks after the CDJs, mixer and any instruments.
  • VJ and video director - the VJ performs the content; the video director runs cameras and IMAG.
  • Up and down riggers - up riggers work at height; down riggers on the floor.
  • Runner - errands and driving, often the first job in the trade.

Most US stagehands work through IATSE, founded in 1893, which counts more than 168,000 members in more than 360 independent locals that each negotiate their own contracts439. IATSE sits on the ETCP certification council438. How much festival and club work is union versus non-union varies widely, and we found no reliable figure.

One more role deserves naming because its absence keeps showing up in disaster reports: the person with clear authority to stop the show. See the safety section.

A note on scale. Electronic dance music tours often carry less audio than rock tours but much more light, video and laser, so the crew skews toward those departments (our observation). Costs have risen sharply since the pandemic, but public numbers are mostly anecdotes from rock acts: Megadeth's Dave Mustaine put his tour's cost at about $45,000 a day "just to sit still"440, and Shinedown's Brent Smith said trucks and buses alone were "six times more expensive" than in 2019441. Billboard Canada's sources said food, lodging and transport had "as much as doubled"442. An accounting firm's advice is that the number of trucks and buses drives "nearly every major expense category"443.

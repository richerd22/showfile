At a rock show, the thing you came to see is making the noise. Four people hit things, the air moves, and the production's job is to make that louder and easier to see. At an electronic show the noise is usually already finished. It was made months ago in a bedroom or a studio, bounced to a file, analysed by software, copied to a stick, and now it is being played back - selected, sequenced, blended and bent, but played back. The performer's hands are on a mixer about the size of a pizza box. Nobody in row 40 can see a crossfader move.

So everything else has to carry the show. Our argument, and it is an argument rather than something any source states outright, is this: once the music became a recording, the show had to be built out of everything that is not the music. The sound system became the instrument, as it already was in Kingston in the 1950s. Light, lasers, haze, video, pyrotechnics, scenery and the architecture of the room took over the job that sweat and visible virtuosity do at a gig. That is why a DJ who carries a few hundred grams of USB sticks can headline a stage that took two weeks, four 70-ton cranes and up to 150 crew a day to build1.

This guide follows that logic through the whole ecosystem: how amplified, recorded, electronic music came to need production at all; the lineage of excess from the Grateful Dead's Wall of Sound to Sphere; the DJ booth and why its players talk over Ethernet; lighting, lasers, video, sound, power, effects and staging; the clubs and festivals that shaped the gear; the artist's rig and the livestream; and the un-glamorous part - roles, paperwork, rigging and the safety rules that were mostly written after someone died. Where things change, we say "as of October 2026". Where the sources disagree, we say that too.

    01How music came to need production

    The short version: For most of history, a show's "production" was the building and whatever flame lit the stage. Then, in roughly eighty years, music learned to be stored (recording), carried (radio), made enormous (amplification), generated by circuits (electronic instruments), and finally performed by playing the stored version back to a crowd. Each step moved the spectacle a little further away from the musician's body and a little closer to the equipment.

    Before electricity: the room was the PA

    Before the 1880s, a performance was as loud as its performers and as bright as its fire. The usual marker for the change is the Savoy Theatre in London, which opened on 10 October 1881 and is described as the first public building in the world lit entirely by electricity, using about 1,200 Swan incandescent lamps fed by a 120-horsepower generator on open ground nearby2. "Entirely" needs a footnote of its own: the first generator was too small, so the stage itself stayed on gas until 28 December 1881, when Richard D'Oyly Carte broke a lit bulb in front of the audience to prove it was safe2. His pitch for electric light was practical rather than poetic - the lamps, he said, "consume no oxygen, and cause no perceptible heat"2. It is an early, well-documented case of a production decision made for the comfort of a crowd, and it will not be the last.

    Sound becomes a thing you can keep

    Edison's phonograph is traditionally dated to 12 August 1877, though the Library of Congress notes that some historians think it was probably built several months later; the patent was filed on 24 December 1877. It recorded on tinfoil that lasted "only a few playings"3. Wax cylinders followed in the late 1880s, and Emile Berliner patented the flat-disc gramophone on 8 November 1887 - in the Library of Congress's phrase, "the lateral-cut flat disc doomed the vertical-cut cylinder"4. Edison did not concede to discs until 19133.

    Broadcasting has a messier birth certificate. Reginald Fessenden's famous Christmas Eve 1906 broadcast from Brant Rock, Massachusetts is disputed: Radio World found no press coverage from 1906 and traced the story to a letter Fessenden wrote in 1932, though a demonstration on 21 December 1906 is documented5. The safer date for scheduled broadcasting is 2 November 1920, when Westinghouse's KDKA in East Pittsburgh read out election returns at 100 watts; the IEEE calls it a world pioneer of commercial broadcasting while noting that other stations claim to be older6.

    Amplification, and the first sound engineers

    The moving-coil loudspeaker arrived in public on 10 December 1915, when Jensen and Pridham's Magnavox was demonstrated in Golden Gate Park, San Francisco, playing music and carrying a speech by the governor7. By 1919 Magnavox speakers carried a speech by President Woodrow Wilson that was reportedly audible "a mile away from the stadium"7. Public address had become a technology, and a technology needs someone to run it.

    For rock and pop, that someone is usually Bill Hanley. He founded Hanley Sound in 1954 in Medford, Massachusetts, is known as "the Father of Festival Sound", and is credited with moving the mixing desk out into the audience - the position we now call front of house - and with using more microphones than was then normal8. He worked Newport in 1965, the night Dylan went electric, and at Woodstock in 1969 his crew covered more than 400,000 people with what was described as one of the most expensive, elaborate and powerful sound systems ever built8. His stated goal still reads like a mission statement for the whole trade: "to give those seated in the last seat in the house the same experience of those seated in the front row"8.

    The Beatles' 1965 Shea Stadium show, played through the stadium's own PA to about 55,000 screaming people, is often cited as the moment it became obvious that touring sound needed its own equipment and its own specialists. That framing is interpretation rather than documented fact, and the pages that describe the show could not be checked for this guide, so treat it as folklore with a good point.

    The instruments go electric

    The theremin is usually dated to 1920, though the Moog Foundation's timeline lists it as "Russia, 1924"9. In Paris around 1948, Pierre Schaeffer and colleagues at the French radio system's Studio d'Essai developed musique concrète - music assembled from recorded sound on tape10. Ten years later, Edgard Varèse's Poème électronique played through 400 loudspeakers at the 1958 Brussels World's Fair10, which makes it, in our reading, one of the first multi-speaker spatial sound installations: a production, not just a piece. In 1964 Robert Moog's transistorized, voltage-controlled modular synthesizer appeared in the US, "programmed with patch cords", with Don Buchla's 100 series developing in parallel on the West Coast; the Minimoog of 1970 was built to be played live9.

    Playback becomes performance

    The decisive step is the one where playing a record in public stops being a substitute for a band and becomes the event. That happened in several places at once, and most of the dates in this part of the story are conventions rather than certainties.

    In 1940s and 1950s Kingston, rival sound systems - mobile rigs of turntables, amplifiers and large homemade speakers - competed for crowds at street dances, and by the mid-1950s they were more popular at parties than live musicians11. Selectors chose the records, DJs toasted over them, and rival systems fought sound clashes with exclusive one-off dubplates11. The culture travelled with Caribbean migration to the UK, and DJ Kool Herc carried its habits, heavy bass included, to the Bronx11. Herc's back-to-school party at 1520 Sedgwick Avenue on 11 August 1973 is the conventional birthday of hip-hop, a date that Rolling Stone itself frames as part "history" and part "mystery"12.

    Disco, house, techno and acid house followed in the same spirit: the New York lofts and discotheques of the 1970s, Chicago's Warehouse (the club "house" music is named after), the Detroit producers of the 1980s, and the UK's "Second Summer of Love" in 1988. The rooms and gear of that era get their own section below. The point here is the shift itself. The Kingston sound system was already the instrument being performed - which makes sound system scale, in our view, the first form of electronic music production.

    Why that created "production"

    Put the pieces together and the thesis falls out (this is our inference, labelled as such). Electric light was a deliberate design choice by 18812. Dedicated live-sound craft existed because of people like Hanley, who needed the back row to hear what the front row heard8. Spatial, multi-speaker presentation existed by 195810. When the thing on stage became a finished recording played by a DJ, the musicians' bodies, instruments and visible effort stopped carrying the show. The spectacle had to come from somewhere else: the scale of the sound system, then light, then video, then the architecture of the booth and the room. Everything in the rest of this guide is a department of that "somewhere else".

    02The lineage of excess

    The short version: Modern electronic spectacle has rock ancestors, and you can trace them through a small number of systems and an even smaller number of designers. The Grateful Dead's 1974 Wall of Sound proved a PA could be enormous, clean and ruinously expensive. Pink Floyd, Kiss, Jean-Michel Jarre, Kraftwerk, Parliament-Funkadelic, Genesis, U2 and the Rolling Stones each proved one more thing, and Daft Punk's 2006 pyramid handed the whole toolkit to dance music.

    The Wall of Sound (1974)

    The Wall of Sound was a public-address system the size of a building, stacked behind the band so that it was both the house PA and the monitors at the same time. Owsley "Bear" Stanley conceived it after his release from prison in 1972 and built it with Grateful Dead sound crew Dan Healy and Mark Raizene and with Ron Wickersham, Rick Turner and John Curl of Alembic1314. (Wikipedia's infobox says 1972 for the design; Stanley's own quoted account says 197315.)

    Its organising idea was separation. Instead of mixing everything into one pair of speaker stacks, it ran six separate systems on scaffolding, with dedicated speakers for each instrument and voice - which kept intermodulation distortion low and let players hear where each sound came from14. There were 11 channels: vocals, guitar, lead guitar, piano, three for drums and four for bass, one per string, split with a quadraphonic encoder14. There was no mixing console for the vocals. Each singer used a matched pair of Bruel and Kjaer microphones wired out of phase into a differential amplifier, so the sound of the Wall arriving at both capsules cancelled out and only the voice sung into the near one was amplified1615. It is a clever trick, and it is also a measure of how badly the Wall wanted to feed back.

Present it as a range and move on.

    A preliminary version debuted at Stanford's Maples Pavilion on 9 February 1973, and every tweeter blew during the first song1415. The full system toured from the Cow Palace on 23 March 1974 to Winterland in San Francisco on 16-20 October 19741513. FOH Online says the best sound reached the first 600 feet and stayed audible for about a quarter mile; Stanley claimed half a mile with no delay towers1315. It died of cost: production costs "spiraled out of control", fuel got expensive and scarce in the 1973-74 crisis, and the crew and its hangers-on grew141315. The band stopped touring after October 1974 and came back in 1976 with something more practical15.

    Whether it was the first line array depends on whom you read. FOH Online calls it "a precursor to line arrays"; Wikipedia calls it the first large-scale line array in modern sound reinforcement, citing a 2001 Mix article we could not access1315. What Hi-Fi? makes narrower and safer claims: it used flown speaker cabinets, now standard, and reached distance with raw power where modern rigs use delay stacks17. Our reading: it was a working proof of several ideas modern PAs rely on, and "first line array" is a widely repeated secondary claim.

    Bear's legacy outlived the hardware. The Owsley Stanley Foundation preserves more than 1,300 of his soundboard tapes and describes his mixing creed - no EQ (move or replace the microphone instead), twelve or fewer mics, and "constructive leakage"18. He died in a car accident in Queensland, Australia, in March 201119. Some of the Wall's amplifiers and cabinets were sold at Sotheby's in October 202114.

    The lesson the Wall teaches, and that the rest of this section keeps re-teaching, is that too big to tour is a real category. It proved that a stadium could hear a band cleanly. It also proved that nobody could afford it.

    Pink Floyd. The Azimuth Coordinator, built by Bernard Speight at EMI Studios, used rotary rheostats and joysticks to pan sound among up to six speakers around a hall; Rick Wright played it live, and a surviving unit has been at the V&A since 2009 (the Wikipedia article describing it is flagged as thinly cited)20. The Wall premiered on 7 February 1980 at the LA Memorial Sports Arena, where opening pyro set a curtain on fire. The wall itself was 31 feet high and 160 feet wide, built from 420 cardboard bricks during the first half and torn down in the finale, with set design by Mark Fisher and animation and inflatables by Gerald Scarfe21. It cost an estimated $1.5 million before the first show; Nick Mason called it "absurdly expensive", and it played only four cities because it had to be set up and left in place22.

    Kiss. Formed in 1973 and known for pyrotechnics, costumes and make-up23. Gene Simmons first set his hair on fire while fire-breathing on New Year's Eve 1973 at the Academy of Music in New York, and estimates he did it "probably six or seven times" over his career24.

    Jean-Michel Jarre. His free open-air concerts set crowd records that are, by nature, estimates. His own biography gives about 1 million for the Place de la Concorde on 14 July 1979, 2.5 million at La Défense in 1990 and 3.5 million in Moscow in 1997; Wikipedia's figures differ for several shows, and nobody has independently verified any of them2526. Later work includes a kinetic laser installation across 12 buildings in Riyadh in 201825.

    Kraftwerk. On the 1981 Computer World tour, life-size mannequins of the band performed during "The Robots"27. The band later moved into 3D concerts and museums, with an eight-album, eight-night retrospective at MoMA in 2012 and a run at Tate Modern in 201327. Whether the masked and helmeted DJ (Daft Punk, deadmau5, Marshmello) descends from those mannequins is a tempting idea that no source we found makes explicitly. File it under inference.

    Parliament-Funkadelic. The Mothership, built of aluminium with a $275,000 budget from Casablanca's Neil Bogart, delivered Dr. Funkenstein to the stage28. The Smithsonian credits the design to George Clinton, Peter Larkin and Jules Fisher and says the original was used only at the end of concerts from 1976 to 198129. It was sold off amid debt in 1982, ended up in a Maryland junkyard and remains lost30. The object in the Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture is a 1990s replica, not the original, whatever some coverage says29. A new, lighter third version landed at ESSENCE Fest in New Orleans on 5 July 202628.

    Genesis and Vari-Lite. The first Vari-Lite VL1s, described as the first digitally controlled robotic spotlights, debuted with Genesis at a bullring in Barcelona in September 1981; the story belongs to the Light section below3132.

    U2. Zoo TV (1992-93) ran 157 shows with stage design by Willie Williams, Trabant cars turned into lighting rigs, and Philips Vidiwalls the band paid $4-5 million for. It cost $125,000 a day to run, margins were about 4-5% at sold-out shows, and almost the whole show was synced to pre-programmed video and audio33. That last detail makes Zoo TV, in our reading, the closest rock ancestor of the timecoded electronic show. The 360 tour (2009-11) put the band under "the Claw", a structure 164 or 167 feet tall depending on the source, built in three copies at £15-20 million each and needing 120 trucks per set; it grossed $736.4 million34.

    Rolling Stones. Steel Wheels (1989-90) grossed $175 million, then the most of any rock tour, with a Mark Fisher set and lighting by Patrick Woodroffe35. A Bigger Bang (2005-07) grossed $558.3 million on a 25-metre Mark Fisher stage, and its free Copacabana show drew about 2 million people36.

    Notice who keeps turning up. Mark Fisher designed The Wall, Steel Wheels, A Bigger Bang, Zoo TV's stadium leg and 360; Jonathan Park worked on The Wall and Zoo TV; Willie Williams did Zoo TV and 360; Jules Fisher built the Mothership and held a 1965 automated-fixture patent213536333432. The family tree is literal.

    Dance music takes the keys

    Daft Punk's pyramid debuted on Saturday 29 April 2006 in Coachella's Sahara Tent, built for about 10,000 and reportedly holding 40,000 or more (a widely repeated figure that traces back to a rumoured number in LA Weekly)37. It was designed by Martin Phillips from off-the-shelf parts - Barco OLite LED panels in a triangular frame, Element Labs VersaTubes and pixel curtains - and Phillips is clear that nothing new or custom was invented. The duo improvised in Ableton Live, lighting cues were largely run by hand, and Daft Punk paid for it partly out of their own advance37. Water & Music credits it with helping to spark an "arms race" in live-show technology. Phillips' own response is the best line in this whole lineage: "You don't go home humming the lights!"37

    There is a neat reversal here, which is our observation rather than anyone's quote. The Wall of Sound was bespoke and collapsed under its cost. The pyramid was assembled from rental-grade products and scaled.

    deadmau5 unveiled his first LED Cube at Coachella in April 2010; Wikipedia calls him "highly influential to the rise of audio-visual shows"38. Tomorrowland's mainstage turned into scenic architecture: the 2016 build was a 120-metre enchanted forest around a 25-metre tree, made from about 780 polystyrene blocks and more than 40,000 man-hours39. The 2025 "Orbyz" mainstage - 45 metres high and 160 metres wide by one account, more than 50 metres tall and 140 metres wide by another - burned down on 16 July 2025, less than 48 hours before opening404142. (More on that fire, and the rebuild, below.)

    Then the venue itself became the show. Sphere in Las Vegas opened on 29 September 2023 with U2, cost about $2.3 billion, and wraps its audience in a 16K by 16K interior LED screen of about 160,000 square feet, with 580,000 square feet of LED on the outside43. Its electronic residencies so far include Anyma (December 2024 to March 2025), a joint Insomniac and Tomorrowland run in 2025, and Illenium in 202643. Dead & Company, the Grateful Dead's successor band, played a residency there from May 2024 to May 202543. Fifty years after the Wall, the lineage literally came back to its starting band. Calling that "full circle" is ours.

    The economics of excess

    Some of the reasons spectacle keeps escalating are sourced. In 2002 the economist Alan Krueger showed concert prices rising 61% over five years while consumer prices rose 13%, and the top 5% of artists' share of concert revenue climbing from 62% in 1982 to 84%. He tied the price rise to falling album sales and downloading and was sceptical that sets and pyro explained it44. Spectacle also runs on thin margins: Zoo TV's 4-5% is the clearest number we have33.

    Phones changed the design brief too. In 2018 Rolling Stone reported designers staging for the audience at home; the show designer LeRoy Bennett put it as "you can't sacrifice your live show for Instagram, but you can take it into consideration"45. Mixmag reported in 2020 that the party brand Elrow valued user-generated Instagram marketing at about €100,000 per show, and that the design firm Wonderworks saw a swing from LED-wall stages back toward big physical scenery46. And the arms race has a cost for everyone else: Water & Music notes that high-end production can be prohibitive for emerging acts, widening the gap with headliners37.

    03The booth

    The short version: A modern DJ booth is two to six media players, a mixer, sometimes an effects unit, a pair of booth monitors and a network switch. The players read tracks that were analysed on a laptop beforehand, share them over Ethernet, and send audio to the mixer, which sends one feed to the room and another to the DJ's own speakers. The gear standardised around one brand's players because a DJ could fly in with nothing but a USB stick and find the same machines everywhere.

    Turntables: the Technics SL-1200

    The SL-1200 launched in October 1972 as a hi-fi turntable - Technics marketed it as "The Middle Class Player System"47. Its parent technology was the SP-10 of 1970, which Technics calls "the world's first direct drive turntable"48. The club deck came later: the SL-1200MK2 of 1979 was, in Technics' words, "the first turntable specifically developed for use in discos", with its pitch control widened and moved "from a knob to a vertical fader", and from the 1980s it became "the de facto standard turntable in the club scene"48.

    The deck won on two counts. Its direct-drive motor kept the platter spinning at the right speed while a DJ pushed the record back and forth, which helped scratching develop47. And the thing is close to indestructible - plenty of 1970s units are still in heavy use47. Its classic pitch range was ±8%; the 2002 MK5G added a ±16% option47. Technics' own brand story credits Kool Herc with using a pair of Technics direct-drive decks at that Bronx party in August 197348; other histories argue about what he actually played, so that one stays attributed to Technics.

    Panasonic discontinued Technics analogue turntables in late 201049, announced a return at CES in January 2016 with the SL-1200G and a limited GAE run of 1,200 units at about US$4,000 each - hi-fi decks, not DJ decks50 - and brought back the DJ model as the SL-1200MK7 in January 2019, described as "a full revival of the feel of the SL-1200MK2"51. Technics says about 3.5 million SL-1200s sold over 35 years48.

    Mixers: rotary, fader and the flagship

    The DJ mixer began as a modified PA mixer. Rudy Bozak, at the urging of the sound engineer Alex Rosner, converted a mono PA mixer into the stereo Bozak CMA-10-2DL, the standard through the 1970s; the UREI 1620 of 1983 drew heavily on Bozak circuitry but used integrated circuits52. Both were rotary mixers - each channel's level set by a knob. The fader layout came from the GLI PMX 7000 (US) and Citronic SMP101 (UK), both 1977, which introduced vertical channel faders plus a horizontal crossfader52. Hip-hop cutting and scratching wanted straight faders; long house and disco blends were happy with knobs52.

    Pioneer's DJM line grew from there. Mixdown dates the DJM-500 to 1995, with effects that synced to tempo; Wikipedia pairs it with the CDJ-500 in 1994, so the year is in dispute5354. The DJM-800 followed in 200453. Allen & Heath's first DJ mixer, the Xone:62, arrived in 199952.

    Rotary mixers then came back as a boutique category. In 2003 the Parisian DJ Deep asked Jerôme Barbé to fix his UREI 1620, which led to the hand-built E&S DJR400; Condesa, Isonoe and others followed52, and Rane's MP 2016 had launched in October 1999 specifically for the house community55. Mixmag Asia's 2021 account of the revival cites the shorter signal path, the status of owning one, and price tags of $3,000 and up56. Condesa's founder argues that typical mixers pass audio through 15 to 20 integrated-circuit stages57.

    The CDJ, from SL-P1200 to CDJ-3000X

    The CD player for DJs began, according to Wikipedia without a primary source, with the Technics SL-P1200 in 198658. Denon's DN-2000F, a rack-mounted controller running two CD transports, was unveiled at NAMM in January 1991 and reached booths in 1992; Wikipedia dates a Denon dual-deck to 1993, which may be a different model5958. Pioneer's CDJ-500 of 1994 is what Pioneer calls "the world's first flat-top CD DJ player"60.

The rest is our inference, but a strong one: the same machine in every club means no learning curve, predictable soundchecks and fewer surprises at 2am. Pioneer DJ's estimated market share was about 60% in 2014 coverage54.

    Who makes it now: Pioneer, KKR, AlphaTheta

    The corporate history matters because the names on the gear changed. In September 2014 Pioneer Corporation agreed to sell 86% of its DJ business to KKR for about ¥59 billion, creating Pioneer DJ Corporation65. On 1 January 2020 that company became AlphaTheta Corporation (the name refers to brainwave frequencies), and the Japanese holding company Noritsu bought it in March 20206667. Since January 2024, new products carry the AlphaTheta brand while older models keep the Pioneer DJ name67. In practice, as of October 2026, booth staff and riders still say "Pioneer" and "CDJs", and the accurate phrasing is "AlphaTheta (formerly Pioneer DJ)".

    The competition moved too. AlphaTheta agreed to buy Serato in 2023; the UK competition regulator found the deal could reduce competition, New Zealand's Commerce Commission blocked it in June 2024, and the Canadian firm Tiny bought 66% of Serato in April 2025686970. On the other side, inMusic - owner of Denon DJ, Numark, Akai and, since 2016, Rane - signed a definitive deal to buy Native Instruments, maker of Traktor, on 8 May 2026, after NI entered preliminary insolvency in January717273. In June the close was still "expected in the coming weeks"74; by August inMusic was talking about the "ongoing integration of Native Instruments" and closing NI's UK company75. That points to a completed deal, but as of October 2026 we have not found a formal closing announcement. Denon's SC6000 (2020) remains the main alternative player, which DJ Mag called "a genuine alternative to the CDJ range"76.

    From timecode vinyl to rekordbox to streaming

    Laptop DJing came in through the turntable. Final Scratch, developed by the Dutch firm N2IT with Stanton hardware, let DJs control computer files with special timecode records in the early 2000s (its exact release date is not confirmed by a primary source)77. Serato Scratch Live followed in 2004 with Rane hardware78. Ableton Live 1.0, released on 30 October 2001, made beat-warped hybrid sets possible79.

    rekordbox, launched in 2009 alongside the CDJ-2000, moved the laptop's preparation work onto the USB stick: tracks are analysed in advance, and cue points, loops and hot cues go to the stick so DJs "don't need to bring a laptop"6154. That single change is why the modern booth looks the way it does.

    Streaming arrived next. As of December 2025, rekordbox supported Tidal, Spotify, Apple Music, Beatport, Beatsource and SoundCloud Go+; Serato the same six but one at a time; Traktor only Beatport and Beatsource; and Denon's Engine OS a similar mix80. Apple Music reached the main DJ apps in March 2025, and Spotify returned to them in September 2025 after pulling out years earlier80. The caveats are practical: most services need internet, offline lockers are capped (Beatport and Beatsource at 1,000 tracks), streaming disables in-software recording, and consumer services carry licensing ambiguity for paid gigs80. Production advice that follows from this (ours): a streaming DJ needs reliable booth internet, and should still carry a USB backup.

    What each box does

    The signal chain is short. Each player reads a track and sends audio to a channel on the mixer. The mixer has per-channel gain, EQ and filters, channel faders and a crossfader, a headphone cue bus so the DJ can preview the next track privately, built-in effects, and at least two outputs: a master (house) output to the room and a booth output to the DJ's monitor speakers, so the DJ can hear without waiting for the room's echo55. An external effects unit sits between mixer and output; Pioneer's RMX-1000 of 2012 split this into Scene FX for builds and breakdowns, Isolator FX for band kills, an X-Pad for drum rolls and Release FX for clean exits81.

    The terms of art, plainly:

    • Library analysis. Before the gig, software scans each track to map its beats (the beatgrid), draws waveforms and stores cue points, loops and hot cues, then exports them to USB61.
    • Quantize. Snaps loops, cues and effects to the nearest beat on the grid, so a button pressed slightly early still lands in time61.
    • Sync. Matches tempo and beat phase between decks using those beatgrids. It is only as good as the grid.
    • Master tempo / key lock. Changes speed without changing pitch; already on the CDJ-50053. Key Shift on the CDJ-3000 moves the key on purpose60.
    • PRO DJ LINK. The network cable between players and mixer that lets one stick feed every deck. Next section.

    Where the tracks come from

    The core instruments of house and techno were commercial flops that became influential second-hand. Roland's TR-808 came out in 198082. The TB-303, designed by Tadao Kikumoto as a bass companion for a drum machine, launched in 1981, sold about 10,000 units and was discontinued in under two years; Phuture's "Acid Tracks" (1987) turned its squelch into acid house83. The TR-909 of 1983, another roughly 10,000-unit failure, was adopted cheaply by Chicago house and Detroit techno producers84. Roger Linn's Akai MPC60 (1988) brought the 4x4 pad grid that later turned up on DJ gear85. Today most tracks are made in software, Ableton Live among the most common.

    Things people get wrong

    • "The SL-1200 was designed as a DJ deck." The 1972 original was hi-fi. The DJ-specific design is the 1979 MK248.
    • "The 2016 revival brought back the DJ turntable." The 2016 models were premium hi-fi. The DJ deck returned with the MK7 in 20195051.
    • "CDJs play CDs." The CDJ-3000 and 3000X have no disc drive6062. "CDJ" is now a legacy name for a USB and network media player.
    • "Pioneer invented the DJ CD player." Technics (1986) and Denon (1991-92) came first. Pioneer claims the first flat-top one585960.
    • "Pioneer DJ is still part of Pioneer." Not since 2015. The company is AlphaTheta6566.
    • "AlphaTheta owns Serato." The deal was blocked; Serato went to Tiny70.
    • "Rotary mixers sound warmer because they're rotary." RBMA's point is that both knobs and faders typically use logarithmic pots; the sound comes from the circuit, not the control52.
    • "Spotify has always been in DJ apps." It returned to the major DJ software only in September 202580.
    • "The 303 and 909 were hits." Each sold about 10,000 units, flopped new and became a legend second-hand8384.
    • "Sync means the DJ isn't doing anything." Sync aligns tempo. Selection, EQ, phrasing and energy are still manual. That one is our opinion, and we're keeping it.

    04Why your CDJs are on a network

    The short version: Almost every system at a modern show talks over the same ordinary Ethernet used in offices. The DJ players use it to share one USB library and beat timing; the lighting desk uses it to carry hundreds of channels of control data; the audio and video systems use it to replace heavy copper cables. Most show-network failures are boring, and so are the fixes: a separate switch for each system, wired connections, and a sensible power-up order.

    Ethernet in plain words

    Ethernet moves data in small addressed envelopes called frames. Each frame starts with the hardware (MAC) address of where it is going and where it came from, says what kind of data it carries, holds a payload of typically 46 to 1,500 bytes, and ends with a checksum that detects corruption in transit86. An IP packet - the thing the internet runs on - rides inside that payload86.

    A hub, the old way, repeats every frame out of every port, like someone shouting every letter to the whole office. A switch learns which device sits on which port and sends each frame only where it needs to go, like a mailroom that knows every desk87. Switches still pass broadcasts to everyone, and they use the Spanning Tree Protocol to stop accidental cable loops from flooding the network87.

    Every device also needs an IP address. Usually a DHCP server - the front desk, often built into a router - hands them out on a lease88. If nobody is at the front desk, devices give themselves a link-local address in the 169.254.x.x range: they pick a number at random and ask around (with ARP probes) to make sure nobody else has it89. Pioneer players do exactly this when there is no DHCP server, which is why a laptop has to be in the same range to see them90.

    The physical rules are just as plain. Copper Ethernet runs a maximum of 100 metres per cable; gigabit needs Cat5 or better (AlphaTheta specifies Cat5e), on the 8P8C connector most people call RJ45919293. Longer runs go on fibre: gigabit multimode reaches 220 to 550 metres (further with better fibre), and single-mode goes to 5 or 10 kilometres92. Power over Ethernet sends power down the same cable, from 15.4 W per port in the 2003 standard up to 90 W in the 2018 one94. VLANs carve one physical switch into several isolated networks, so audio, lighting and DJ traffic can share hardware without stepping on each other9596.

    PRO DJ LINK: the answer to "why does a CDJ need Ethernet?"

    PRO DJ LINK was announced with the CDJ-2000 in September 2009. MusicRadar's report described an "industry-first" feature letting up to four players and rekordbox connect "by LAN connection" so that one USB or SD card could supply music to every linked player61.

    Here is the problem it solved, in our words (AlphaTheta has never published its design rationale). A DJ wants to plug one stick into one player and load any track on any deck. Each deck also needs to know where the others are in the beat, so sync, quantize and tempo-synced effects work. That means moving whole files and metadata between machines, many-to-many, plus a steady stream of tiny timing messages. MIDI cannot carry files, and USB is a one-host-to-one-device connection. Ethernet was cheap, standard, two-way, worked with any switch and laptop, and needed no setup thanks to link-local addressing. The protocol's actual traffic matches that reading: small broadcast packets for beats and status, and larger requests for files and database lookups979899.

    What LINK does, per AlphaTheta's own setup guide: it connects compatible players and mixers with rekordbox on a computer or phone "via wireless LAN or LAN cable", and enables beat sync, an on-air display, quantize, key sync, library sharing, and PRO DJ LINK Lighting, which sends phrase data to lights through a DMX interface93. Up to six players are supported, though players released before 2019 are limited to four93. What it does not do: "It isn't audio over ethernet," as DJ TechTools puts it - the sound still goes to the mixer over audio cables - and it does not turn a player into a MIDI controller100.

    Why you need a switch. Two players can share one cable. A third needs a switch; a cheap 10/100 switch with five or more ports is enough100. AlphaTheta's guidance is to power the switch or router first and let it finish booting before connecting DJ gear, to use a separate router for each LINK system in large venues, to avoid loops, and to use Cat5e or better93.

    How the players find each other. Under the hood, devices announce themselves by broadcasting to UDP port 50000 and negotiate player numbers; plugged into a mixer channel port, a player accepts the number the mixer assigns97. Mixers send keep-alives about every 1.5 seconds and CDJs about every 2 seconds97. Beat packets go out on UDP 50001, one per beat, carrying tempo, pitch and the position within the bar; CDJ-3000s add absolute-position packets every 30 milliseconds98. Track metadata, artwork, beatgrids and waveforms come from a small database server on each player, queried over TCP99.

    The famous problems.

    • Player Conflict. Two players with the same number. The one that joined last complains, and the number cannot be changed while media is inserted100. LINK is only established when the player number on screen is lit white93.
    • Boot order. On a network with DHCP, if the server is not up before the players power on, they fall back to 169.254 addresses and a laptop on the venue range cannot see them. Power-cycling a player after the server is up usually fixes it90.
    • Wi-Fi. Deep Symmetry, whose tools depend on this network working, is blunt: "Wi-Fi has latency and collision issues that destroy the ability to reliably interact with DJ gear"90.
    • Old or odd models. The CDJ-850 and CDJ-350 have no Ethernet, and some all-in-one units cannot exchange LINK data with CDJs100101.

    The people who documented it. AlphaTheta has never published the protocol. In summer 2015 James Elliott of Deep Symmetry wanted to sync his light shows to CDJs: "I broke out Wireshark and after staring at packet captures over a weekend..."102. The resulting analysis became the de facto public documentation, and a whole ecosystem of third-party tools grew on it - Beat Link, Beat Link Trigger and libraries in Python, Go, C++ and TypeScript102101. Beat Link's own disclaimer: "This is in no way a sanctioned implementation of the protocols ... Use at your own risk!"101

    The official answer. AlphaTheta's rekordbox Lighting mode shipped in February 2018, using phrase analysis to build light shows automatically and outputting one DMX universe through the RB-DMX1 interface - which uses a 3-pin XLR, a connector the DMX standard technically prohibits103104. Its PRO DJ LINK Bridge software sends real-time track and playback data from the players to lighting, video and effects systems, with partners including Avolites, ChamSys, Resolume, SoundSwitch and TC Supply105. In September 2026 rekordbox and Bridge added official SoundSwitch support, replacing an older integration that relied on Ableton Link tempo sync106. TC Supply's ShowKontrol is the other established bridge from CDJs to lighting desks107.

    Denon has its own equivalent, StageLinQ, whose public documentation is likewise community-built108. And Ableton Link (2016) syncs beat, phase and tempo between apps over wired or wireless networks with no master: "No single participant is responsible for maintaining the shared session tempo"109110.

    DMX, RDM, Art-Net and sACN: how the lights get their orders

    DMX512 is the language lights speak. Created in 1986 by USITT and now the ANSI E1.11 standard (current edition 2024 per ESTA), it sends 512 channels - a universe - one way down a daisy-chained cable, each channel a number from 0 to 255104111. A moving light might use a dozen or more channels: one for pan, one for tilt, one for dimmer, one for the gobo wheel, and so on. The physical rules: 250 kbit/s on RS-485 signalling, five-pin XLR (the standard prohibits three-pin, which everyone uses anyway), no more than 32 devices per line, no more than 400 metres, and a 120-ohm terminator at the end. A full universe refreshes about 44 times a second104.

    RDM (ANSI E1.20) adds two-way traffic on the same cable, so a console can find fixtures, set their addresses and read back their status remotely104111.

    The arithmetic explains why lighting moved onto Ethernet (this is our reasoning from the cited limits). One DMX cable carries 512 channels to at most 32 devices. A festival rig of moving lights, LED fixtures and pixel-mapped strips can need dozens or hundreds of universes. A single Ethernet cable carries many universes, runs over fibre, uses commodity switches and can talk back. The usual chain is console to Ethernet to a node, which converts back to DMX cable for the last daisy chain to the fixtures.

    Timecode and clock: "where" versus "how fast"

    Timecode says where you are in a show (hours:minutes:seconds:frames). Clock says how fast to go. SMPTE LTC carries timecode as an audio signal - 80 bits per frame at 24, 25 or 30 frames per second - so it can travel over any audio cable116. MIDI arrived in 1983117; MIDI Timecode (1986) packs the same timing into small quarter-frame MIDI messages118, and MIDI beat clock sends 24 pulses per quarter note plus start, stop and continue messages119. OSC, proposed at CNMAT in Berkeley in 1997 and published as spec 1.0 in 2002, is the flexible networked successor that many visual and lighting tools use120.

    In practice (our reading of how the tools are used), a fixed headline show with lights, video and pyro cued to a playback track runs on timecode. A DJ set with no fixed timeline drives lighting and video from beat data instead: PRO DJ LINK, StageLinQ, Ableton Link or MIDI clock. Bridges like ShowKontrol can generate SMPTE timecode synced to the decks, which blurs the line121.

    Audio and video over IP: Dante, AES67, AVB, NDI

    Dante, developed in 2006 by Sydney's Audinate, carries uncompressed multichannel audio over ordinary IP networks - up to 1,024 channels per link, with latency as low as 150 microseconds122. Yamaha's design guide spells out the switch rules: non-blocking gigabit, quality-of-service priority for clock sync then audio, IGMP snooping for multicast, managed switches, and Energy Efficient Ethernet turned off, because "it can be said that EEE is incompatible with real time applications"96. AES67 (2013) is the interoperability bridge between Dante and rival audio networks123. AVB, a set of IEEE standards, and its pro-audio certification Milan (launched 2018, backed by L-Acoustics, Meyer Sound, DiGiCo and others) are the alternative favoured by some loudspeaker makers124125. NDI, revealed by NewTek in 2015 and now published by Vizrt, moves video between computers and cameras over the network at about 100 Mbit/s for HD, or 8-20 Mbit/s in its compressed HX form126127.

    Why it matters at a show (our summary): one fibre pair replaces a multicore snake, the same audio can feed front of house, monitors, broadcast and recording without splitters, and VJ laptops, media servers and cameras can swap feeds without new cable runs.

    • Power the switch first, then the gear; on DHCP networks, the server before the players9390.
    • Check player numbers before doors93.
    • Only one LINK program per network adapter; give a laptop a second Ethernet adapter if it must join90.
    • No loops93.

    05Light

    The short version: Show lighting grew from theatre spotlights and 1960s liquid light shows into rigs of motorised fixtures that pan, tilt, change colour and project patterns on command. The modern moving light dates to Genesis in a Barcelona bullring in 1981. At an electronic show, light and haze do much of the work that a band's physical presence does elsewhere, and a lighting console sends all of it orders over the DMX network described above.

    A short history of making light move

    Remotely moved lights are older than rock. A 1906 patent describes a carbon-arc lantern moved by hand with cords, and a 1925 design used electric motors128. ETC's history adds a 1928 patent describing what we now call pan and tilt, George Izenour's Yale students building robotic lights in the 1950s, and a 1965 automated-fixture patent from Jules Fisher32.

    The counterculture contributed the other half. Liquid light shows emerged in the early 1960s; Bill Ham's kinetic lighting at the Red Dog Saloon in 1965 is described as possibly the first psychedelic light show129. The Joshua Light Show, founded by filmmaker Joshua White, was the house show at Bill Graham's Fillmore East from 1968 to 1970129130. American shows of that kind ran on overhead projectors with liquids sloshed in clock-face glasses, sometimes seven operators on 30 or more projectors129.

    The moving light as a touring product came from a sound company. Showco, in Dallas, had worked with Genesis since 1971. In autumn 1980 one of its partners, Jack Maxson, suggested a colour-changing fixture could "add two more motors and it moves"; the prototype was shown to Genesis in London that December, and the band's manager Tony Smith paid for it and suggested the name Vari-Lite131. The first Vari-Lites premiered on 25 September 1981 at the Plaza de Toros Monumental in Barcelona, the opening night of the Abacab tour131. How many fixtures that tour used depends on who you ask: ETC says Genesis ordered 55, while PLSN says the 1982 tour used more than 15032131. The VL4 of 1988 was "the first practical wash light"32. The honest claim is not that the VL1 was the first remotely moved light, which the patents rule out, but that it was the first commercially successful touring system.

    Europe followed. Clay Paky, founded in Bergamo in 1976, released the Golden Scan in 1987 or 1988 (sources differ), the first luminaire to use stepper motors instead of servos132128. High End Systems' Intellabeam of 1989 let production companies buy automated lights instead of renting them from Vari-Lite, which dominated through rental133. Martin Professional of Aarhus began with a smoke machine adapted from a coffee maker in 1978 and launched the MAC 500/600 in 1997134. Robe, in the Czech Republic, celebrated 30 years in 2024135 and agreed to buy the console maker Avolites in 2023136. The Summa HTI of 1985 is described as the first moving head controlled by DMX512128.

    The beam fixture that defines the electronic-show look is Clay Paky's Sharpy, launched in 2010 with a 189 W lamp and a beam that looks "more like a laser ray"; the PLASA awards judges said they "never thought we'd see a 190W light that we could use in Wembley Arena"137. Sources then moved to LEDs and lasers. Radiohead's 2008 tour reportedly used only LED instruments138. Claypaky calls its 2019 Xtylos "the first moving head light with a laser light source", and Cameo marketed its 2024 Oron H2 as the "world's first IP65 hybrid moving head with phosphor laser engine"139140. Both are manufacturer claims; nobody neutral has settled which "first" is real.

    Strobes went the same way. Martin's Atomic 3000, a 3,000 W xenon flash tube, "has for many years dominated the market for strobe effects in concerts"; its LED successor uses 234 LEDs at 740 W141.

    What the fixtures do

    Moving lights come in four families128:

    • Spot - a hard-edged beam that can project patterns (gobos) sharply.
    • Wash - a soft, wide beam for colouring areas.
    • Beam - a very narrow, intense parallel shaft, the Sharpy family; with haze, these draw the geometric lines in the air the genre is known for.
    • Hybrid - one fixture doing several of the above.

    They are either moving heads, where the whole optical unit swivels on a yoke, or older scanners, which bounce the beam off a moving mirror - faster, but with a smaller range128. Common features include pan and tilt, dimmer and shutter, zoom, focus, iris, rotating gobo wheels, colour wheels, CMY colour mixing, prisms and frost128.

    The conventional fixtures are still around138:

    • Ellipsoidal or profile (the Source Four, the Leko): a hard-edged spot with a gate for gobos or irises, doing "the bulk of lighting roles in the theater".
    • PAR can: a sealed-beam lamp in a can; the number is the lamp diameter in eighths of an inch, so a PAR-64 is 8 inches.
    • Fresnel: a stepped lens that throws a wide, soft-edged beam.
    • Followspot: moved during the show by an operator to track a performer.
    • LED fixtures: usually red, green and blue emitters mixed additively, with no gels and less power.

    Why is it called a gobo?

    A gobo is a pattern template placed in the focal plane - the gate - of a projecting fixture, such as an ellipsoidal or a moving-head spot, so the light comes out as a sharp image: leaves, a logo, a breakup texture142. It goes in upside-down and back-to-front, because the lens flips the image142. Steel gobos are tough but need little "bridges" to hold floating parts, like the middle of an O; glass gobos need no bridges and give the finest detail, but break; dichroic glass can reproduce colour photographs; plastic works only in cool LED fixtures142. Patterns placed after the lens instead of in the gate are called flags or cucoloris142.

    As for the word: nobody knows where it comes from. Merriam-Webster says "origin unknown", with a first known use in 1923 - and in that first sense a gobo was a dark panel used to block light from a film or television camera, not a pattern in a spotlight143. Wiktionary says "uncertain; possibly from go-between", and notes that writing it as an acronym "may be a folk etymology"144.

    The popular explanations - "goes before optics", "goes between optics", "graphical optical blackout" - are backronyms. Wikipedia's etymology section traces "goes before optics" to a 2016 computer-graphics book and "graphical optical black-out" to a 3D-measurement web page, not to any lighting history, and cites theatre texts saying the acronym reading is recent142. A story that the word began as "go between" in Edison's studios rests on a forum post142. Popular explainers repeat the acronyms while citing only Merriam-Webster, which says it does not know145.

    The logic settles it: the 1923 sense was a film-set panel with no optics involved, so "goes before optics" cannot be the original meaning. The honest answer is a film-studio word of uncertain origin, attested from the 1920s, with "go-between" as the most plausible guess. In film work a gobo still often means a flag or blocking panel. In stage lighting it means the little etched disc.

    Atmosphere: fog, haze and the smoke detector problem

    Light in clean air is invisible until it lands. To see a beam, you need particles in the air for it to bounce off.

    • Fog is a dense, visible cloud, made by vaporising glycol or glycol-water fluid in a heated exchanger146.
    • Haze is a fine, near-invisible suspension whose whole job is to scatter light so beams and laser lines show up. It is made by atomising mineral oil or vaporising water-based haze fluid, and the fluids are not interchangeable146.
    • Low fog is fog chilled - by ice, a compressor, liquid CO2 or dry ice - so it hugs the floor146.

    The ESTA standard ANSI E1.5-2003 says water-glycol and glycerin fog fluids are "not likely to be harmful" to healthy workers and audiences, while a 2005 study linked cumulative mineral oil and glycol exposure to respiratory effects in workers; dry ice and liquid nitrogen add frostbite and oxygen-displacement hazards146.

    Haze also sets off smoke detectors, "particularly photoelectric detectors"147. The standard fix is to agree it with the venue in advance; venues may isolate detectors in the event space and post a fire watch - a person whose only job is to act as the smoke detector - sometimes for a fee147. A UK town council's hire conditions are a good model of the paperwork: isolation only by prior written agreement and only by the venue's fire safety manager, a dedicated fire watch in each isolated room, the hirer paying for any false-alarm call-out, haze stopped if it obscures escape routes, and detectors re-enabled and tested at the end148.

    Consoles, programming and previs

    The touring console families, as of October 2026:

    • MA Lighting (Germany, founded 1983): grandMA in 1997, grandMA2 in 2008, grandMA3 presented in January 2018149.
    • Hog (Flying Pig Systems' Wholehog, launched at PLASA in September 1992; Hog 4 in 2012 under High End; now ETC)150133.
    • Avolites (London, founded 1976 by touring crew; Tiger Touch in 2011; owned by Robe since a 2023 agreement)136.

    How a show is run splits two ways (common practice rather than a sourced rule). A headliner production is often timecoded: cues are pre-programmed and locked to the playback. A club night or an unscripted DJ set is usually busked: an operator plays the lights live on faders and buttons, reacting to the music. Most real shows mix the two.

    Who does what in lighting

    The lighting designer creates the look while "keeping in mind issues of visibility, safety, and cost", and on smaller tours may also program and operate the desk154. On bigger shows the jobs split: a programmer builds the show file (palettes, cues, timecode), and an operator runs it nightly. Electronic shows add a laser operator and sometimes a lighting director who adapts the design to each festival's house rig. That division is standard industry practice rather than something our sources define formally.

    06Lasers

    The short version: Almost every laser you see at a show is powerful enough to damage eyes, which is why lasers are the most regulated light at the party. In the US, any show laser above a low power limit can only be used under an FDA variance, and sweeping beams across the crowd needs a separate, specific approval. Most of Europe has no variance system and relies instead on general safety law, risk assessments and, in some countries, permits and certified laser safety officers.

    Classes, in plain terms

    Lasers are classed by how much harm they can do (IEC 60825-1, summarised here)155:

    Class What it means Old US class
    1 / 1M Safe in normal use; 1M is safe unless viewed through magnifying optics I
    2 / 2M Visible only, up to 1 mW; relies on the blink reflex II (up to 1 mW)
    3R Visible, up to 5 mW; low risk, though the safe exposure limit can be exceeded IIIa (up to 5 mW)
    3B Up to 500 mW; direct viewing is hazardous, diffuse reflections generally not; key switch and interlock required IIIb (5-500 mW)
    4 Above 3B; skin burns, eye hazard even from diffuse reflections, fire risk IV (above 500 mW)

    The working concept behind all of it is the MPE, or maximum permissible exposure: the highest exposure considered to carry a negligible chance of damage, typically set at about a tenth of the dose with a 50% chance of injury155. MPE depends on wavelength and exposure time, so there is no single safe number to quote.

    ILDA, the industry association, focuses its rules on Classes 3B and 4 because Class 2 and 3R lasers are "too dim for most shows"156. In other words: if it looks like a show, it is probably 3B or 4.

    The US variance system, as of October 2026

    US federal law treats an entertainment laser as a "demonstration laser product" - one made or promoted "for purposes of demonstration, entertainment, advertising display, or artistic composition"157. Rule 21 CFR 1040.11(c) says every such product must comply with the requirements for Class I, IIa, II or IIIa158. Class IIIa tops out at 5 mW visible, which is where practitioners' talk of the "5 mW line" comes from.

    Anything stronger can only be used under a variance - a formal, numbered exemption granted by the FDA's Center for Devices and Radiological Health under 21 CFR 1010.4159. A variance can be granted if the applicant shows equal protection by other means, or that the product's purpose could not be achieved under the standard; it comes with conditions and an end date, can be withdrawn immediately for an imminent hazard, and its number must appear on the product label159.

    How it works in practice, per the FDA's own page160:

    • Show producers are treated as manufacturers, and assembling a show from an existing laser can count as making a new product.
    • The paperwork is Form 3147 (variance application) and Form 3640 (Laser Light Show Report), plus Form 3632 (Product Report) from the projector manufacturer. Buy a certified projector that already has a Product Report, and you need only the 3640 and 3147.
    • Shows may begin only after the approval letter arrives.

    Laser Notice 55 (2007) added that variances renew automatically one year at a time if the annual report is filed, and that holders can add certified Class IIIb or IV projectors without amending the variance - except projectors used for audience scanning or with invisible emissions, which still need CDRH approval161. Incidents of public exposure must be reported161. ILDA's summary puts the annual report on Form 3636, due by 1 September for the July-June period156. ILDA also notes that manufacturers generally cannot deliver Class 3B or 4 projectors before the buyer's variance is approved, that an FDA accession number only means a report was received rather than approved, and that FDA jurisdiction is triggered by admission fees, paid performers or paid functions - a laser in your own living room is outside it162.

    The standard beam rules. Under normal variance conditions, beams stay at least 3.0 metres (about 9 feet 10 inches) above any surface the audience stands on and at least 2.5 metres to the side or below156162. The operator must be in direct personal control with a view of every beam path, with accessible emergency stops, secondary safety cables on projectors and written procedures156.

    Audience scanning - deliberately sweeping beams across the crowd - needs an FDA-approved audience-scanning variance. Without one, ILDA says any audience exposure is "very hazardous and illegal"156. ILDA describes the approved methods as beam-splitting through gratings, high-inertia scanning with mirror balls or rotators, and low-inertia galvanometer scanning with multiple scan-fail safeguards, and says most require measuring the beam's irradiance at the closest audience position162. ILDA's FAQ quotes a single power-density figure for audience scanning; we are not repeating it, because the real limit depends on wavelength and exposure time, and a single number misleads163.

ILDA's summary adds that the FDA expects state and local agencies to be notified, and states add their own layers: Arizona requires a trained laser safety officer, New York requires a state laser operator licence, and ILDA lists inspection or notification rules in Illinois, Massachusetts, Texas and Florida162156.

    Europe and the UK

    The UK has no variance or permit system. The Health and Safety Executive's guidance says duty holders "are required by law" to assess the risk, that clients must make sure laser contractors are competent, and that controls include enclosures, blanking plates, scan-failure safety, emergency stops, barriers, competent operators and following aviation guidance169.     When it goes wrong

    The best-documented laser disaster in electronic music happened outside Moscow on 5 July 2008, at the Aquamarine open-air festival in Kirzhach. Rain moved the party into tents, and lasers meant to be pointed at the sky ended up partly in the crowd. Reuters reported 29 people treated at two Moscow eye hospitals, and an ophthalmologist told Kommersant "they all have retinal burns", with vision loss of up to 80% in some171. ILDA's incident page puts the total at 35, suspects a pulsed laser, and reports a later claim that sight was restored in all but four172. Our reading: a misuse case - sky lasers brought indoors without being re-set - rather than a failure of the technology.

    ILDA's list includes other cases: up to 19 people partially blinded in Bulgaria in 1999, one person losing sight in one eye at a Spanish nightclub in 2014 (settled for €80,000), 65 reported vision losses in Kolhapur, India, in 2022, and one case of photokeratitis in South Africa in April 2026. Injuries at Tomorrowland in 2009 were attributed to crowd members' laser pointers, not the show172.

    The LSO, sources and software

    A Laser Safety Officer evaluates hazards and sets controls, and needs a working grasp of MPE and the law. ILDA "strongly suggests" anyone responsible for laser shows take an LSO class; its show-specific course meets the ANSI Z136.1 criteria for show-relevant areas, while general LSO courses often skip audience scanning and aviation173. There is no single federal LSO certification in the US156.

    Inside the projector, diode lasers are now the most common source, with optically pumped semiconductor (OPSL) lasers in some high-end units and diode-pumped solid-state (DPSS) mainly in smaller ones174. The manufacturer Laserworld dates the switch to green laser diodes around 2013, which made projectors more compact and efficient than the temperature-sensitive DPSS greens that came before (manufacturer framing)175.

    Shows are programmed in software such as Pangolin Beyond, which can be driven from a PC, a lighting console, SMPTE timecode, MIDI or DMX/Art-Net176. ILDA maintains the technical standards underneath, including the .ild file format and the DB-25 projector connector, and published a revised network stream specification in July 2025177. ILDA itself was founded in 1986178.

    07Video and LED walls

    The short version: An LED wall is a grid of panels, each covered in tiny red, green and blue LEDs, fed by a processor that splits one video signal across hundreds of panels. Its sharpness is set by pixel pitch - the gap between pixels - and how it looks on camera is set by refresh rate and scan. Behind it sits a media server or VJ laptop, either improvising to the DJ or playing content locked to timecode.

    From Diamond Vision to the blue LED

    Stadium screens came before LED walls. Mitsubishi's Diamond Vision, first demonstrated at the 1980 Major League Baseball All-Star Game in Los Angeles, used cathode-ray tubes; Sony's JumboTron debuted at Expo '85 in Tsukuba, Japan, with about 450,000 pixels of flood-beam CRT179. They were built from CRTs because there was no practical blue LED, and without blue there is no full-colour LED pixel179.

    That changed in the early 1990s, when Isamu Akasaki and Hiroshi Amano at Nagoya and Shuji Nakamura at Nichia produced bright blue LEDs, a problem that had resisted solution for about three decades. They shared the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics180. The Nobel citation talks about white lighting; it took the rest of the industry to point out that blue also "brought ... full-color LED displays into practical use"181.

    Touring caught up fast. U2's PopMart tour (1997) carried an LED screen roughly 150-170 feet wide, made of 150,000 pixels by SACO Technologies of Montreal; Mark Fisher proposed spacing the pixels further apart to make the screen bigger, and it was meant to be the largest video screen in existence at the time182. By the 360 tour (2009-11), U2's expanding LED screen of hexagonal segments on a pantograph hung inside the Claw34. Daft Punk's 2006 pyramid was built from Barco LED panels37, with video handled by XL Video183. SACO, which made PopMart's pixels, also made the tiles for Sphere's interior screen, a nice through-line182184.

    How a wall works

    Pixel pitch is the distance in millimetres from the centre of one pixel to the centre of the next; smaller pitch means more pixels and a sharper picture up close185. Planar's rule of thumb is pitch in millimetres times 10 equals the comfortable viewing distance in feet, so a 3 mm wall looks good from about 30 feet185. Do not treat that as law: for a 2 mm wall, different manufacturers' formulas give minimum distances anywhere from about 6 to 16 feet186. Touring outdoor walls tend toward coarser pitches - a typical rental panel is 5.9 mm at 5,000 nits187 - while broadcast and close-up work goes much finer.

    How LEDs are packaged affects durability and contrast. SMD (surface-mounted device) solders tiny LED packages onto the board188. COB (chip on board) mounts the LED chips directly on the module, giving higher contrast, a tougher surface and tighter pitch189188. GOB (glue on board) is SMD sealed under epoxy for protection189.

    Brightness is measured in nits, one candela per square metre189.

    Refresh rate is how many times per second the LEDs redraw the frame - typically 1,920, 3,840 or 7,680 Hz - which is a different thing from the video's frame rate189.

    Scan describes how many rows of LEDs one driver chip lights in turn. At 1:16 scan, only one line in 16 is lit at any instant; a lower scan ratio leaves time for higher refresh and finer brightness control189190.

    Why your phone sees bands. LEDs are dimmed by pulsing them on and off very fast. A camera's rolling shutter can catch four pulses on some rows of its sensor and three on others, which shows up as dark bands, worst on dark content190. Cameras catch this more easily than eyes; the fixes are higher refresh rates or genlock, which syncs the camera and wall to a common timing reference189. Brompton's ShutterSync adjusts the LED timing to suit a given camera shutter191.

    Moiré is the shimmering interference pattern you get when a camera's sensor grid and the wall's pixel grid are slightly offset or differently spaced189. Bit depth sets how many brightness steps each colour gets: 256 at 8-bit, 1,024 at 10-bit, 4,096 at 12-bit189.

    The signal chain. A media server or computer sends video to an LED processor (Brompton, Megapixel, NovaStar and others), which slices the image and sends each piece over Ethernet or fibre to a receiving card inside each panel. The receiving card is "the link between the LED processor ... and the driver ICs", and applies brightness, gamma and colour calibration189192193194. Each panel also needs power, and the processor dictates the order in which data cables run from panel to panel195.

    Diagram 5 · Video to the wall

    Hanging it. Walls are either flown from truss and chain hoists - which needs rated rigging points, certified riggers and a longer load-in - or ground-stacked, which needs a level floor, rear bracing and, outdoors, ballast and wind calculations. One supplier's line is worth repeating: "A calm forecast is not a design basis"196. Not every panel can curve, and flown versus stacked affects weight limits and panel choice197.

    Media servers, VJs and the software in between

    Where VJing came from

    08Sound

    The short version: Dance music has always been bass-first, from Kingston street dances to Berghain. Two philosophies now coexist: horn-loaded club systems that try to do as little to the signal as possible, and line arrays shaped by digital processing that dominate festival main stages. Signal runs from the DJ mixer to a processor, amplifiers and speakers, and a limiter at the end means pushing the mixer into the red makes things worse, not louder.

    A short history of the dance-music PA

    Sound systems became popular in 1940s Kingston: trucks loaded with generators, turntables and big speakers for street parties, with custom-built rigs from the late 1950s and some systems later boasting 30,000 watts or more of bass11. The scholar Julian Henriques describes the culture's bass as "enveloping, immersive, intense"11.

    In New York, the sound engineer Alex Rosner built the system at David Mancuso's Loft and pushed Bozak to build its DJ mixer21752. Richard Long started out repairing systems for Rosner, went on to install more than 300 of his own, and in 1977 designed the Paradise Garage system, which he treated as his showroom and kept modifying; its "Levan Horn" bass enclosure was named after the club's DJ, Larry Levan217. Long won Billboard's award for best disco sound design in 1980 and died in 1986217.

    In the UK, Turbosound was co-founded in 1978 by Tony Andrews, Tim Isaac and John Newsham; Andrews and Newsham left after its 1992 sale to Harman and formed Funktion-One218. Funktion-One builds high-efficiency horn-loaded speakers rather than processing-heavy line arrays. Andrews: "The whole approach with audio for me is to do as little as possible with the signal"218. Its systems are associated with Berghain and Glastonbury's dance areas, among others218. Void Acoustics, founded in 2002, installed in clubs including DC-10 Ibiza and Tresor Berlin (per its own history)219.

    On the touring side, L-Acoustics, founded in France in 1984 by the physicist Christian Heil, introduced V-DOSC in 1992 on what it called Wavefront Sculpture Technology - though Heil did not invent line-array theory itself220. d&b audiotechnik was founded in 1981 near Stuttgart by Jürgen Daubert and Rolf Belz, adopted digital processing in 1997 and introduced its Cardioid Subwoofer Array in 2004221. Meyer Sound, founded in Berkeley in 1979 by John and Helen Meyer, moved the amplifier into the speaker with its self-powered MSL-4 in 1994222.

    Line arrays and cardioid subs

    Line arrays solve an interference problem. Clustered speaker boxes pointed the same way interfere with each other, producing peaks and dips across the audience (comb filtering). A properly aligned vertical column of boxes behaves more like one even wavefront, reducing hot and dead spots and saving truck space223. Our observation: in dance music, the two philosophies split by room. Horn-loaded systems hold the clubs; processed line arrays hold the big festival stages.

    Sub-bass is there partly because it makes people move. A McMaster University study switched inaudible very-low-frequency bass on and off every two minutes during an electronic concert, and found dancing increased by 11.8% when it was on224.

    The trouble with bass is that it goes everywhere. At 30 Hz the wavelength is longer than 37 feet, and a single 18-inch subwoofer measured nearly the same in front and behind225. Cardioid subwoofer arrays fix this with timing. In a gradient array, two boxes sit about a quarter-wavelength apart and the rear one is delayed and polarity-flipped, so sound cancels behind and adds up in front; the price is a few decibels of forward output, some punch, and floor space225. An end-fire array lines subs up front-to-back and delays the front ones most, with the same goal226. The payoff is less low-end bleed onto the stage and toward neighbouring stages223 - which, for a DJ booth surrounded by subs, matters a great deal.

    The chain, and why redlining doesn't work

    A DJ mixer has separate house and booth outputs55. The house feed goes to a front-of-house console or straight to a loudspeaker processor, which applies the crossover, EQ, delay and limiting, often built into the amplifier itself; L-Acoustics' LA4X, for example, is four channels of 1,000 W with filters, up to a second of delay per channel and real-time speaker protection227. Then amps, then speakers.

    That limiter is the reason redlining fails. Because the venue's processor caps the maximum level, pushing the mixer into the red only flattens and distorts the music; stacking the mixer's limiter on top of the house limiter strips out transients and tires ears228229. The advice from the booth is consistent: watch every stage of gain, raise all of them a little rather than one a lot, and keep headroom - "headroom is your friend", as one festival backline tech put it229230. There is a physiological trap too: DJs develop a temporary hearing threshold shift over a set, so the room seems to get quieter and the gain creeps up231228.

    At a DJ-only show (our reading), there is often no "mix" at front of house in the band sense. The engineer mainly guards the system limiter and the overall level, and the booth monitors are the DJ's monitor mix. On bigger shows a system engineer arrives first to measure the room, assemble and tune the PA, and walks the coverage zones during the show232.

    How loud is too loud

    Weighting matters. A-weighting (dBA) mimics how ears respond at low levels and cuts bass heavily; C-weighting (dBC) mimics hearing at high levels and keeps the bass. At 100 Hz, an A-weighted reading is about 20 dB lower than a C-weighted one, so a 100 dBA limit leaves far more room for bass than a 100 dBC limit233. LAeq is an A-weighted average over a stated period.

    The WHO standard. The World Health Organization's Global Standard for Safe Listening Venues and Events (March 2022) recommends no more than 100 dB LAeq over 15 minutes, plus live level monitoring, better acoustics and sound systems, free hearing protection, quiet zones, and staff training234235.

    Limits by country (from a 2019 working-group snapshot published by Live DMA; check the current law before relying on it)236:

    Country Audience limit Notes
    Switzerland 100 dB(A), hourly average Never above 125 dB(A) momentary
    Belgium (Flanders, Wallonia) 100 dB(A) over 60 min at FOH Tiers at 85/95/100 trigger earplugs, displays, trained staff; Brussels adds 115 dB(C)
    France 102 dB(A) and 118 dB(C), over 15 min C-weighted cap targets bass
    Germany 99 dB(A) recommended, over 30 min 135 dB(C) peak
    Netherlands 103 dB(A) over 15 min Industry agreement

    Two observations. A 15-minute average is stricter in practice than a 60-minute one, because a loud peak-time quarter-hour cannot be averaged away. And A-weighted limits understate exactly the bass-heavy exposure dance music produces, which is what C-weighted caps are for.

    Workers are covered separately. In the UK, music and entertainment came under the Control of Noise at Work Regulations from April 2008, with the Sound Advice guidance for venues including nightclubs238. In the US, OSHA's limit is 90 dBA averaged over eight hours with a 5 dB exchange rate, and hearing conservation starts at 85 dBA; NIOSH recommends a stricter 85 dBA with a 3 dB exchange rate237. Under OSHA's rule, 100 dBA allows about two hours233.

    What it does to DJs. In a 2007 study of 30 DJs in Curitiba, Brazil, club levels measured 93 to 110 dB(A), 27% already had measurable hearing loss at high frequencies before their shift, and 66.7% reported tinnitus right after231. Studies cited in the same paper found tinnitus in 74% of Scottish DJs231.

    Earplugs work. In a randomised trial at an Amsterdam festival, temporary hearing loss appeared in 8% of ears with earplugs versus 42% without, and new tinnitus in 12% versus 40%239. The DJ Anne Savage, quoted by the HSE, noted that many DJs say they cannot mix in earplugs but can wear them the rest of the night238.

    09Power

    The short version: A show runs on venue power or generators, fed through heavy cables to a main distribution box and then to smaller ones at each stage. Audio gets its own clean circuit, separate from lights and video. Single-pole connectors are connected ground first and disconnected ground last, and hum is fixed on the signal side - never by defeating the safety ground.

    Sources and sizing

    Power comes from the grid (shore power) or from towable generators, often synchronised in pairs for redundancy at greenfield sites240. Planning guidance is consistent: build 20-30% headroom above the expected peak, keep circuits at or below about 80% of their rating, allow for the inrush when amplifiers and moving lights switch on, and leave three-phase hookups to licensed electricians240. Generators prefer to run at 50-75% of their rating; below about 30%, diesels suffer "wet stacking"241.

    Power flows from the source to a main distro (distribution box, often called the company switch), then over feeder cable to sub-distros at each stage241. The connectors on that feeder are typically cam-lok single-pole connectors, one per conductor. The US electrical code requires them to be listed and locking, colour-coded (green for ground, white for neutral), and connected only by a qualified person or with interlocks: ground first, then neutral, then the hot legs, and the reverse to disconnect242. A common entertainment colour code for 120/208 V adds black, red and blue for the three phases (forum-sourced)243.

    The codes, and what each one covers

    In the US, temporary event power sits in several articles of the National Electrical Code (NFPA 70). Section numbers vary by edition, and jurisdictions adopt different editions, so check with the local authority:

    • Article 520 covers theatres and permanent stage structures, including those at fairs and similar venues244.
    • Article 525 covers portable structures at carnivals, circuses, fairs and similar events - rides, tents, concessions - with rules such as 15 feet of clearance from overhead lines and extra-hard-usage cords245244.
    • Article 590 covers temporary installations generally; section 590.6(A) requires GFCI protection on 125 V 15, 20 and 30 A temporary receptacles241.
    • Section 406.13 covers single-pole separable connectors like cam-loks242.

    ANSI E1.19 is not a distribution or connector standard. It is ESTA's recommended practice for using Class A GFCIs - ground-fault protectors for personnel - in entertainment, covering circuits up to 100 A; Class A devices trip above 6 milliamps, and European RCDs do not meet the Class A definition246247.

    Hum, ground loops and keeping audio clean

    Run audio and lighting on separate circuits, and video too where possible: older dimmers and some LED fixtures inject noise, and separation also means a tripped lighting circuit will not kill the sound240. A ground loop happens when equipment has a second path to ground through a signal cable's shield, and it shows up as hum248. The fixes are balanced lines, isolation transformers, a single reference ground point, lifting the ground on the signal side only, or digital and optical links248240. The rule everyone repeats, for good reason: "Never remove or defeat ground pins on plugs"240.

    A small battery backup on the booth's players, mixer and laptop is common practice to ride through a brief dropout or a tripped circuit. We found no authoritative source for it, but nobody has ever regretted one.

    10Fire, air and confetti

    The short version: The effects that mark the drop - CO2 jets, confetti, flames, sparks, pyro and kabuki drops - are all governed by fire codes and local permits, and in the US by two NFPA standards in particular. Most deadly incidents follow one pattern: sparks indoors, near a flammable ceiling or acoustic foam. And "cold spark" machines are not cold; fire officials treat them as pyrotechnics.

    CO2 jets and cryo

    A CO2 jet does one thing: it opens a valve, fires a column of liquid carbon dioxide, and closes again. The white plume is "frozen humidity", gone within seconds, leaving nothing on the floor249. The usual setup is one siphon tank, one hose and one jet; valves are rated to at least 1,450 psi, and plumes reach about 8-20 feet on small jets, 35-55 feet on DMX valve jets and 75-90 feet on multi-valve units, shorter in heat and dry air249. One supplier lists 15-20 feet of audience clearance, notes that every type of CO2 effect needs a permit in Las Vegas, and recommends giving effects their own DMX universe so noise on a shared line cannot cause a misfire249.

    The hazard is the gas, not the cold. CO2 is about 1.5 times as dense as air and collects in low spots. NIOSH and OSHA set a shift-average limit of 5,000 ppm, and NIOSH lists 40,000 ppm as immediately dangerous to life or health; symptoms run from headache and dizziness to convulsions and asphyxia, plus frostbite from the liquid250. Industrial gas guidance puts alarm thresholds at below 19.5% oxygen or above 0.5% CO2, and stresses that CO2 is a health hazard in itself, not only an oxygen displacer251. The body's urge to breathe is driven by CO2 rather than by low oxygen, so a person in an oxygen-poor pit can pass out with little warning, and readings can be fine at chest height and unsafe at floor level252. The scenario to worry about (our emphasis) is CO2 dumped into a low, enclosed, badly ventilated space: an orchestra pit, a sunken stage, a basement club.

    Confetti and streamers

    Electric confetti cannons fire on a button or trigger, with ranges of up to 12 metres; one major maker's tissue confetti is listed as certified biodegradable (in named colours) and flameproof to the German B1 standard253. Not every "biodegradable" claim carries a certificate, and water-soluble confetti only dissolves when soaked and agitated; if it dries first, it can stick to floors and become a slip hazard254.

    Flame effects

    A stage flame unit's safety logic is a model of careful engineering. On one widely used design, one DMX channel arms it and a second fires it; it arms only when the safety channel sits between 40% and 60%, then counts down for three seconds while it self-checks, and any other value drops it back to safe mode255. A tilt sensor shuts it down if it is knocked, there is a latching emergency stop, and the operator must be able to see every unit; the danger zone is a 3-metre radius with 6 to 10 metres clear overhead, and the flames reach 3 to 6 metres255. Practitioners add their own belt and braces: flame units on their own universe, a manned emergency stop on the power, and a dead-man handle that enables the effect while DMX only handles the timing - because DMX is one-way and has no error checking256.

    Pyro, and the standards that govern it

    In the US, NFPA 1126 covers pyrotechnics before a proximate audience - musical, theatrical and similar productions, indoors and out - and, according to a published summary of its 2026 edition, now requires every operator to be a "pyrotechnic professional"257. NFPA 160 covers flame effects before an audience, from design to defueling; the same kind of summary says its 2026 edition makes the operator, not production management, responsible for deciding how long safe removal takes258. States and cities adopt NFPA 1126 into their own law259, and local fire departments issue specific permits for proximate pyro260. Federal rules define categories such as display fireworks and "theatrical flash powder" in kits of an ounce or less261. State licences vary; North Carolina's 1.4G operator licence, for example, requires the holder to be 21 or older, complete 8 hours of training, have lead-operator experience on three shows and pass an exam262.

    "Cold spark" machines are not cold

    The machines sold as "cold spark" feed a powder of roughly 95% titanium and 5% zirconium into a tube heated to around 560 C until it glows, then blow it out through a nozzle; the patent claims the falling sparks cool to a touch-safe temperature263. Fire officials disagree with the framing. Mesa, Arizona's fire department says "there is no such thing as 'cold sparks' or pyrotechnic effects that are safer than others", notes that titanium powder can self-ignite in air as low as 482 F, and says NFPA 1126's definitions include cold spark technology264. West Milford, New Jersey, requires a permit, operator certification, insurance, 10 feet of clearance from combustibles, a Class D metal-fire extinguisher and possibly a paid fire watch265. The accurate description is "a lower-temperature spark effect, still regulated".

    Kabuki drops

    A kabuki drop releases a curtain or banner on cue so it falls away to reveal what is behind it. Electromagnetic release units can hold loads up to about 50 kg and are triggered remotely266.

    Who fires the effects, and how

    Pyro and flame are run by licensed or certified operators under a local permit257262. Effects are usually cued over DMX on a dedicated universe, with a separate arm or safety channel and a manual enable held by a person who can see the device255249256. Some crews put a second operator with a better view on the safety while the main desk handles timing256. For DJ shows, where the track structure is not fixed, the drop is usually fired by hand, or armed in advance and released by hand; full timecode suits pre-built show files. That last point is our inference.

    The fires that wrote the rules

    On 20 February 2003, at The Station nightclub in West Warwick, Rhode Island, pyrotechnic gerbs set off by a touring band's manager ignited polyurethane acoustic foam around the stage. One hundred people died and 230 were injured; the club had no sprinklers and was over its licensed capacity of 404267. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology found conditions at head height in the middle of the room became lethal "in less than 90 seconds after ignition of foam", and recommended sprinklers in all new nightclubs and existing ones over 100 occupants, a ban on indoor pyro in nightclubs without sprinklers, a ban on non-fire-retarded foam as a finish, and designing on the assumption that the main exit is blocked268. The NFPA amended its Life Safety Code within months to require sprinklers in existing nightclubs over 100 occupants and crowd managers267.

    The pattern repeats:

    • Club Pulse, Kočani, North Macedonia, 16 March 2025 - 59 dead in the first reports, rising to 63 by October 2025 as the injured died. Sparks from stage pyrotechnics ignited acoustic foam on the ceiling, and investigators later found the club's operating licence was forged269270.
    • Le Constellation, Crans-Montana, Switzerland, New Year's 2026 - 40 dead in the first counts and 41 by February 2026, with about 115 to 119 injured; the preliminary cause is sparklers on champagne bottles held too close to the ceiling, with acoustic foam under examination271272.
    • Formosa Fun Coast, Taiwan, 27 June 2015 - 15 dead and about 500 burned when coloured cornstarch powder blown over a crowd at a party with DJs ignited. Not pyro, but an effect aimed at an audience273.
    • Tomorrowland, July 2025 - the Orbyz mainstage caught fire during a final pyrotechnic rehearsal two days before the festival, and no one was hurt41. A Belgian newspaper later reported, citing sources close to the investigation, that an accidental ethanol spill during subcontractors' testing of the stage's fire bowls may have caused it; that is unconfirmed, and Tomorrowland has not addressed the cause274.

    The lesson (ours, from the pattern): the device is only half of it. Finishes, sprinklers and exits decide whether a spark becomes a disaster.

    11The stage itself

    The short version: Big electronic stages are steel structures from a handful of specialist builders, with scenery layered on top and the DJ booth as the visual centre. They are built very wide, not deep, which means a lot of ballast and a lot of wind engineering. The 2011 Indiana State Fair collapse is the reference case for what happens when that engineering fails.

    The builders

    • Stageco was founded in Belgium in 1985, growing out of stage work for the Rock Werchter festival; it builds stages, ground support, delay towers and temporary structures for clients including the Rolling Stones, U2, AC/DC and Metallica275.
    • Mountain Productions, founded in 1979 in Pennsylvania, supplies staging, roof systems, mobile stages, truss, risers and delay towers, with offices in Europe as well as the US276.
    • Star Events (UK) built the VerTech roof used at many UK festivals; as of 2011 its spans carried up to 7.5 tonnes each277. Its domain now redirects to a for-sale page, so its current status is unclear.

    What an electronic mainstage takes

    The best numbers come from the builders and promoters themselves.

    • EDC Las Vegas 2013, kineticFIELD - an owl with video-screen eyes whose wings closed around the DJ booth between sets: 480 feet wide, more than 335 tons of scaffolding and structure, about 1,968,000 pounds of ballast, more than 2,000 lighting fixtures and over 2 MW of power, in a festival with a reported $35 million production budget278.
    • EDC Las Vegas 2014, "The Cathedral" - 120 metres wide and up to 30 metres tall, in black steel; seven containers from Belgium and 27 trailers from Colorado, a seven-day build worked overnight because the steel got too hot to touch in the daytime, and test-built in Belgium that March279.
    • EDC Las Vegas 2017, kineticFIELD - a modular design with a 220-foot touring centre section extended to about 440 feet in Las Vegas; about two weeks to build with 50 supervisors, up to 100 local stagehands a day, 40 structure trucks, four 70-ton cranes and 125 boom lifts1.
    • Tomorrowland 2025, the emergency rebuild - after the mainstage fire, a replacement 70 metres wide and 8 metres high with about 700 LED cabinets went up in somewhere between one and two days, using parts of Metallica's touring rig airlifted from Austria41280281. The build time depends on who is counting: under 24 hours, 36 hours, or under 4841280281.

    The Tomorrowland rebuild, in our reading, shows how much of a mainstage is bespoke scenery. Touring LED and ground support could stand in for it overnight, at about half the width and a fraction of the height.

    Things that move

    Moving scenery and lights run on entertainment automation: variable-speed chain hoists, load cells and control consoles such as TAIT's Kinesys family282, or large cable-winch rigs like KINETIC lights, whose installations include ABBA Voyage and Hakkasan Las Vegas283.

    When the structure fails

    On 13 August 2011, a gust of about 59 mph hit the stage roof at the Indiana State Fair before a Sugarland concert. Seven people died284. The engineering review found "inadequate capacity of the lateral load resisting system": the roof was required to withstand 68 mph, its concrete-barrier ballast could resist only about 25 to 43 mph, and the guy lines and connections were too weak; there had been no engineering review of the rig as built, and no clear protocol for delaying a show284. Its long tail runs through the rest of this guide: the Event Safety Alliance formed in response to it, and still runs panels on the collapse285286.

    What we could not source: specific DJ booth and riser design numbers - heights, isolation from bass vibration, deck stability - or scrims and projection surfaces. They are listed under open questions at the end.

    12Clubs, festivals and the rooms that shaped the gear

    The short version: A handful of rooms decided what dance-music production sounds and looks like. The New York lofts and discos of the 1970s made the sound system the headliner; London, Manchester and Berlin carried that idea into the superclub; and UK and US law pushed rave culture into licensed venues and enormous festivals. Today a venue's installed "house" package is what every DJ and tour negotiates against.

    The rooms

    The Loft, New York (1970). David Mancuso's first "Love Saves the Day" party was on 14 February 1970 at his home at 645-647 Broadway, for about 100 people; it was invitation-only, sold no alcohol, charged $2 and gave away organic food287. The sound was audiophile home hi-fi scaled up - McIntosh and AR amplifiers, Klipschorn speakers, later a cluster of JBL tweeters over the dance floor - with Alex Rosner as engineer287. Mancuso avoided pitch control, and in the late 1970s stopped beatmatching altogether and played records in full287. A 1972 police raid for unlicensed cabaret led to a city ruling that private parties could run if nothing was sold, which "later benefited the Paradise Garage and other private clubs"287.

    Paradise Garage, New York (1977-1987). Michael Brody's club at 84 King Street was modelled on the Loft and is credited as the first club to put the DJ at the centre288. Richard Long's custom system - his showroom, constantly modified - made it famous217. Justin Berkmann, who later founded Ministry of Sound, told NPR it was "the best sound system ever made in a nightclub", and said Larry Levan constantly adjusted bass and treble to stop ears tiring289. Berkmann also says the rumour that the Garage system went to Ministry is false; Ministry's was a facsimile289. The lease ended in 1987, and the building was demolished in 2018288.

    Studio 54, New York (1977). The theatrical lineage, as opposed to the hi-fi one. Jules Fisher and Paul Marantz designed the dance floor and rigging, with confetti, snow and fog effects, a movable lighting bridge added in 1978 and a fly system in 1979290. Richard Long installed sound there too217. Its first incarnation closed in 1980 after the founders pleaded guilty to tax evasion and the club lost its liquor licence290.

    The Warehouse and Music Box, Chicago. The Warehouse at 206 South Jefferson Street opened in 1977 under Robert Williams; "house" music takes its name from the club, via Williams' "house" party posters and, per one history, a shortening of the club's name291. Frankie Knuckles left in late 1982; the owners' next club, the Music Box (1983-88), had Ron Hardy as resident291.

    The Haçienda, Manchester (1982). Factory Records' club opened on 21 May 1982 with a Ben Kelly interior, financed by Factory, New Order and Tony Wilson; New Order's bassist Peter Hook later said it lost up to £18 million292. Its "Hot" night in July 1988 brought Ibiza-style acid house to Manchester; it lost its licence and closed in 1997292.

    Ministry of Sound, London (1991). Opened in a disused bus garage in Elephant & Castle on 21 September 1991 and inspired by the Garage, it spent £500,000 each on its sound system and on soundproofing, and added a 64-speaker Dolby Atmos system in 2016293. Wikipedia repeats a claim that the room was tested to 156 dB without leakage; treat that as lore.

    Fabric, London (1999). Opened 29 October 1999 in Farringdon with three rooms, each with independent sound; Room One's "bodysonic" floor uses 400 bass transducers so the floor itself vibrates294. Islington Council revoked its licence in September 2016 after two drug-related deaths; it reopened under strict new conditions in 2017294.

    Berghain, Berlin (2004). Opened in December 2004 in a former power plant, with Panorama Bar upstairs; its first Funktion-One "Dance Stack" went in in 2005 and was replaced with an updated Funktion-One system in 2023295. A German court designated it a cultural institution in 2016, cutting its tax rate, and phone cameras are stickered at the door295.

    Two production philosophies run through these rooms (our reading). The hi-fi listening-room lineage - the Loft, the Garage, Ministry, Berghain - prizes a custom, tuned sound system. The theatrical lineage - Studio 54, later Las Vegas - prizes rigging, effects and spectacle. And licensing has repeatedly decided which rooms survive: the Loft's 1972 ruling, Studio 54's liquor licence, the Haçienda in 1997, Fabric in 2016.

    Rave, and the laws that followed it

    The UK's "Second Summer of Love" began in 1988 and peaked in 1989, from clubs like Shoom to orbital raves around the M25296. The state responded in stages. The 1990 Entertainments (Increased Penalties) Act raised fines for unlicensed parties from £2,000 to £20,000 and allowed up to six months in prison296. The Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994 went further. Its section 63, still in force, defines music at an outdoor night-time gathering to include "sounds wholly or predominantly characterised by the emission of a succession of repetitive beats", and lets a police superintendent order people to leave once 20 or more are present in England and Wales (originally 100, still 100 in Scotland); later sections allow seizure and forfeiture of sound equipment297. Autechre responded with a track built on 65 drum patterns, so that nothing repeated298. The argument that this pushed rave culture into licensed superclubs is common and plausible, but the law's text does not say so; treat it as interpretation.

    In the US, the RAVE Act failed in 2002, then passed in 2003 as the Illicit Drug Anti-Proliferation Act, attached to an unrelated bill. It extended the federal "crack house" statute to places used "whether permanently or temporarily" for drug use, with civil penalties of $250,000 or twice gross receipts per violation299300. Its chilling effect on harm reduction is documented: the drug-checking group DanceSafe says promoters fear liability and often will not advertise drug checking, and Electric Forest ordered its booth closed in 2015, even though a 2017 Department of Justice clarification said drug education and free water do not violate the Act301.

    The festival boom

    • Electric Daisy Carnival. Insomniac's EDC debuted in 1997 at the Shrine Expo Hall in Los Angeles; after the 2010 death of a 15-year-old at the LA Coliseum it went 18+, and it moved to Las Vegas Motor Speedway in 2011. Promoter-reported attendance was 525,000 in both 2023 and 2024302, and over 525,000 again in 2025303.
    • Ultra Music Festival. First held on 13 March 1999 on Miami Beach for about 10,000; about 165,000 attended over three days in 2022304305. Its city licence at Bayfront Park runs through 2027 at a $2 million rental fee rising 4% a year305. Wikipedia reports the 2026 edition as the first major US festival powered by batteries; we could not confirm that beyond Wikipedia304.
    • Tomorrowland. First held in Boom, Belgium, in August 2005 for about 10,000; its mainstage has followed an annual theme since 2009 and reached 270 metres wide in 2022306. In 2025 it reported more than 400,000 visitors across two weekends, 16 stages and 15,000 crew281.

    All of these are promoter or media figures, and they count attendance across days, not individual people.

    The house package and the Vegas superclub

    A venue's house package - its installed PA, lighting, video and DJ booth - is what a touring DJ or production advances against on the rider. The Las Vegas residency clubs show the top end. Hakkasan at the MGM Grand, as documented in 2014, had more than 13,400 square feet of 6 mm LED, mostly Robe fixtures plus Sharpys, and a ChamSys console running 64 DMX universes, with a data gateway that lets emergency lighting take over the rig307. Omnia at Caesars Palace opened in 2015 at a reported $107 million, with a kinetic chandelier of eight rings on 21 winches weighing about 22,000 pounds and an L-Acoustics system308309. XS at the Wynn added pyro, lasers and a 14,000-LED DJ booth in 2015; Wikipedia reports a planned closure from September 2026 for renovation, which we could not confirm independently310.

    That emergency-lighting detail at Hakkasan illustrates a general rule (our generalisation): a club's lighting network must hand over to house lights when the fire alarm goes. The fire rules themselves are covered under effects above; one example of how they spread is Massachusetts, where a 2004 law after The Station fire required sprinklers in nightclubs with occupancy of 100 or more and added criminal penalties for blocked exits and unpermitted pyrotechnics311.

    13The artist's rig, the stream and the rights

    The short version: "DJ set" and "live set" sit on a spectrum, from improvised clip-launching to shows locked to timecode, and each point on it needs different production. Recording and streaming a set is easy technically and complicated legally: Twitch has a label licence for live DJ streams, YouTube scans everything, and royalties for the tracks a DJ plays still depend on recognition technology that is being rebuilt as of 2026.

    Beyond two decks

    Ableton Live's Session View is the backbone of most live electronic sets: a grid of clips in tracks and scenes, where "clips can be played at any time and in any order", each track plays one clip at a time, and a scene launch fires a whole row312. Ableton's Push 3 controller can run Live standalone, without a laptop313.

    Stem separation - splitting a finished track into drums, bass, vocals and the rest in real time - went from novelty to standard in about four years:

    The bridges between them keep improving. In 2017 Pioneer announced that CDJs would send real-time waveforms and track data over PRO DJ LINK to ShowKontrol, which can generate SMPTE timecode synced to the decks and trigger Art-Net, TCNet and MIDI cues121. CDJ-3000 firmware added support for ShowKontrol and PRO DJ LINK Bridge in May 2021319.

    "We all hit play"

    The longest-running argument in the genre has a date. In June 2012, deadmau5 posted "we all hit play" on Tumblr: "i just roll up with a laptop and a midi controller and 'select' tracks n hit a spacebar", and "anyone with minimal knowledge of ableton and music tech in general could DO what im doing"320. It was reported on 25 June 2012 as a reply to the reaction to his comments about button-pushing DJs321. Read with this guide's thesis in mind, it is a confession of exactly why the production exists: if the music is played back, the show has to be built from everything else.

    Recording and streaming

    Pioneer's flagship mixers record and stream directly through the DJM-REC app; the DJM-A9 (2023) can keep the microphone off the recording, and its two USB ports let two DJs swap computers during back-to-back sets322. The DJM-V10 (2020) is likewise DJM-REC compatible323.

    The curated livestream format came from two brands. Boiler Room was founded in London in March 2010 as a weekly webcast; ticketing company DICE bought it in 2021 and sold it to Superstruct Entertainment in January 2025, which drew artist cancellations over the parent company KKR's investments324. Cercle, founded in 2016, streams sets from heritage sites, beginning at the Eiffel Tower325.

    The rules, as of October 2026:

    • Twitch. In June 2020 a wave of copyright takedown notices hit streamers, and Twitch moved from muting clips to removing them and issuing strikes; DJ sets were listed as not allowed326327. In June 2024 Twitch announced its DJ Program, licensed by Universal, Warner, Sony, independents and Merlin. It covers live DJ streaming only; Twitch splits the music cost with most streamers 50/50 out of channel revenue; non-monetising DJs pay nothing; and the opt-in applies to the whole channel, so Twitch suggests a separate DJ channel328. The DJ category launched on 8 August 2024329. Archived VODs are not covered and can still be muted330. Those are the 2024 launch terms; we could not confirm whether they have changed since.
    • YouTube. Every live stream is scanned for third-party content, and a match can replace the picture, pause or end the stream. A licence alone does not prevent this; the rights owner has to add your channel to their Content ID allowlist331. Content ID itself lets rights owners block, monetise or track matched material, country by country332.
    • Mixcloud Live. Licensed through direct deals with the three majors and more than 250 independents; it needs a Pro subscription, pays artists through audio fingerprinting, and archives audio only, with no video333.

    Production advice that follows (ours): plan the archive separately from the live stream, and remember that a simulcast to a platform without a licence carries the same unlicensed audio.

    Booth etiquette

    The norms are consistent across sources:

    • Arrive two or three songs before the previous DJ finishes, and know the club's equipment334.
    • End on a long, loopy track the next DJ can mix out of, and do not close with a huge hit before the headliner334.
    • Carry a backup on USB, because PRO DJ LINK can fail; turn the booth monitors down at handover so the incoming DJ sets their own level334230.
    • In a back-to-back, do not coach, do not hog the mixer, and do not EQ your partner's track while they are mixing in335.
    • Keep headroom; redlining does not make the room louder229.

    Firmware is part of etiquette now. The CDJ-3000 has had a steady stream of updates since 2020, and in December 2024 Pioneer posted a notice asking owners to update319. Advancing a show should confirm the exact player model and firmware, and whether a booth mixing CDJ-3000s and 3000Xs links properly; we found no primary source on that compatibility.

    Getting the music paid

    Live performance royalties are paid from setlists. The UK collecting society PRS collects fees from venues and promoters and pays four times a year, but cannot allocate royalties without a setlist; PRS paid £68.5 million in live royalties in 2024336. In the US, ASCAP OnStage and BMI Live let writers report their own performances337. Nobody writes a setlist for a two-hour DJ mix, so recognition technology fills the gap: PRS works with DJ Monitor, which "tracks when and where electronic music is played"338.

    Pioneer's KUVO platform is the other route. It partnered with Canada's SOCAN in 2017 to supply club play data339, closed its consumer features in 2022340, and pivoted to rights data after AlphaTheta invested in DJ Monitor in 2023341. On 21 May 2026 AlphaTheta acquired DJ Monitor's technology and assets to make KUVO a hub for club performance data and music recognition for rights organisations342343. As of October 2026 that platform is consolidating, not finished. The practical advice for producers (our reading): register your works, upload them to the recognition services, and do not rely on DJs filing setlists.

    14Who does what

    The short version: The tour manager owns the money, the schedule and the people. Production, sound, lighting, video, lasers, rigging and effects crews sit under that umbrella, joined on show day by the venue's own staff. Some of those jobs - riggers, laser operators, pyro operators - need certificates or licences; most are learned on the job.

    The roles with solid public definitions:

    • Tour manager. Usually hired freelance per tour by the agent or artist manager. Builds the tour budget (crew pay, per diems, lodging, transport, equipment, visas, rehearsals, commissions), advances each show, settles the fee with the promoter after the show, pays per diems, prepares the next day's schedule and guest list, and handles passes344. A road manager handles one act's details; one person can do both jobs344.
    • Front-of-house and monitor engineers. FOH runs the audience mix; monitors runs what performers hear345. At a DJ show, as noted above, FOH mostly guards the limiter.
    • System engineer. Arrives first, measures the room, assembles and tunes the PA, and walks the coverage during the show232.
    • Lighting designer. Creates the look within the limits of visibility, safety and cost, and on small tours also programs and runs the desk154.
    • Riggers. Hang the chain hoists that lift truss, LED walls and scenery from the building's structure. The head rigger often marks points on the floor and works out bridle lengths by trigonometry346. Certified in the US through ETCP (Arena and Theatre Rigger) and in the UK through the PLASA National Rigging Certificate346347.
    • Laser operator. Must be "a trained, competent operator" in direct personal control who can see every beam path (or has a spotter), with emergency stops always within reach156.
    • Pyro and flame operators. Licensed or certified under state and local rules; NFPA 1126's 2026 edition, per a published summary, requires "pyrotechnic professionals"257.

    The rest are standard practitioner definitions that public sources do not formally pin down, so treat them as trade usage:

    • Promoter - takes the financial risk on a show, rents or partners with the venue, pays the artist, and splits what is left.
    • Agent - represents the artist and sells dates. The talent buyer works for the venue or promoter.
    • Production manager - owns the technical advance, vendors, crew calls and production schedule.
    • Stage manager - runs the stage and the changeovers on show day.
    • Programmer and operator - build the lighting show file, then run it nightly.
    • Backline tech - at an electronic show, looks after the CDJs, mixer and any instruments.
    • VJ and video director - the VJ performs the content; the video director runs cameras and IMAG.
    • Up and down riggers - up riggers work at height; down riggers on the floor.
    • Runner - errands and driving, often the first job in the trade.

    15The paperwork

    The short version: Every show is negotiated in advance and documented on paper. The advance confirms what everyone is bringing; the rider says what the artist needs; the stage plot and input list tell the crew where everything goes and how it connects; the day sheet runs the day; and the settlement reconciles the money afterward. No standards body governs any of it, but good practice is consistent.

    The advance

    The advance is "the planning conversation that happens before the event", in which artist, venue, promoter and production confirm the details345. A tour manager's advance confirms the rider, contacts, load-in and soundcheck times, set times, support acts and curfews, and the results go into a tour book344. A club-level checklist runs: contacts with phone numbers; load-in, soundcheck and backline (send the plot and input list, and agree what the venue supplies); promotion; tickets and door; and payment - who pays, and when353.

    16Rigging

    The short version: Rigging is how heavy things are hung safely above people: chain hoists from rated points in the building, or ground-supported towers and roofs outdoors. Hoists built to the D8+ standard can hang loads over people without extra safeties, standard D8 hoists cannot, and outdoor roofs are governed by an engineering standard whose importance the 2011 collapses made painfully clear.

    Chain hoists ("motors") attach to the building's structure with steel wire rope and shackles, then lift truss, LED walls and scenery346. A dead-hang point drops straight below a beam; a bridle spans two beams so the hook falls between them346. Weak link analysis - finding the lowest-rated component in the chain - is described as the most common rigging assessment346.

    The German hoist standard has two levels that matter. A standard D8 hoist is not designed to suspend loads over people unless a secondary safety is fitted. A D8+ hoist has double brakes and higher safety factors and can suspend over people without one, which also spares a rigger the climb to fit secondaries and the risk of fitting them wrong. The choice should follow a project-specific risk assessment356.

    Outdoors, ground support - towers and roofs standing on the ground rather than hanging from a building - is governed in the US by ANSI E1.21, "Temporary Structures Used for Technical Production of Outdoor Entertainment Events", which sets minimum design and performance parameters and does not cover fire or egress; the current edition is 2024357. The Event Safety Alliance lists ESTA standards for rigging, outdoor ground-supported structures and temporary special-event structures358.

    The reference failure is again Indiana in 2011: the engineering review found the rigging "wasn't built strong enough to meet state building codes". It was required to withstand 68 mph gusts and came down in gusts of about 59359.

    We could not source, and so do not state, ESTA's specific hoist and truss standards, typical arena point loads, or exactly who signs off a rigging plot. In practice the house rigger approves a tour's rigging plot against the building's limits; check the specifics with ESTA and the venue.

    17Safety, and what it cost to learn it

    The short version: Most of the rules in this guide were written after someone died. The recurring lessons are about crowd density and site geometry, structures rated for the weather, and - over and over - having someone with clear authority to stop the show in time. The UK keeps that knowledge in the Purple Guide; the US in Event Safety Alliance guidance and ANSI standards.

    Crowds

    Love Parade, Duisburg, 24 July 2010. Twenty-one people died and 652 were injured. A 240-metre tunnel and underpasses fed a single ramp that was effectively the only way in and out; police turned arrivals back by loudspeaker while people kept pushing in from behind. Autopsies found all deaths came from crushed rib cages, and a 2012 scientific analysis described "crowd turbulence", not a stampede360. The trial ended in 2020 without a verdict, and the Love Parade was permanently cancelled the day after the disaster360. The vocabulary matters: "crowd crush" and "crowd turbulence" are accurate; "stampede" blames the victims for physics.

    Astroworld, Houston, 5 November 2021. Ten people died of compression asphyxia, the youngest aged 9; about 300 were treated on site355. A mass casualty event was declared at 9:38 p.m., and the show ended around 10:10 or 10:15361. Plaintiffs' experts allege the site was planned at 5 square feet per person instead of the fire code's 7, with no density monitoring in the area where all ten were fatally injured, and that the safety lead had written beforehand: "there is no way we are going to fit 50k in front of that stage" - these are allegations, not court findings362. A grand jury declined to indict the headliner and five others355.

    Structures and weather

    Indiana State Fair, 13 August 2011. Seven dead. Sources differ on injuries: some give more than 40, while the Event Safety Alliance's own history and Live Design in 2026 give 58285363. Beyond the engineering failure, one review found preparedness "inadequate" and the emergency plan "not fully developed", and another cited "an ambiguity of authority" over postponing the show364. It was the third weather-related outdoor roof collapse in North America in about three weeks285.

    Pukkelpop, Belgium, 18 August 2011. A sudden storm brought down a stage, a large tent and screens with up to 60,000-65,000 people on site, and the festival was cancelled. Early reports said three dead, then five; injury counts ranged from 40 to more than 70365366. Five is the commonly cited final toll, but we could not verify it from an official source.

    Who keeps the knowledge

    The Event Safety Alliance formed in response to Indiana, led by Linkin Park's tour manager Jim Digby, and debuted at the Indiana Statehouse in April 2012, modelling itself on the UK's Purple Guide285. Its founding date is honestly fuzzy: its origins page says the group formed in early 2012, while it marked its 15th anniversary in 2026, which counts from 2011285363. Both are defensible - the cause was 2011, the organisation was 2012. Its Event Safety Guide first appeared in February 2013 and now runs to 39 chapters; as of August 2026 it was developing a second version and an OSHA 10 course for live events285358363. ANSI ES1.9-2020, approved in July 2020, covers crowd management367.

    The Purple Guide, originally published by the UK's Health and Safety Executive and taken over by the Events Industry Forum in 2012, is subscription-based, non-prescriptive and runs to 45 chapters, from crowd management and medical to weather, temporary structures and special effects368. The ESA traces it to the aftermath of Hillsborough in 1989285.

    Lasers, effects and fire

    Covered in their own sections above. The short reminder: no US laser show above 5 mW without an FDA variance, no audience scanning without a specific one, and outdoor beams need an FAA review filed about 90 days ahead156. Indoor sparks near combustible finishes are the pattern behind the deadliest club fires268269271.

    Harm reduction

    In November 2019 a New South Wales coroner investigating six MDMA-related festival deaths of people aged 18 to 23 made 28 recommendations, including a drug-checking trial "as soon as possible" and an end to drug dogs; the state government opposed pill testing369. In the UK, a new Home Office licensing requirement stopped on-site drug checking in 2023, until the drug-checking charity The Loop received the first licence in May 2024370. In the US, DanceSafe (founded 1998) was integrated on site at TomorrowWorld, which the Drug Policy Alliance called a first for a major promoter371 - against the background of the liability fears described under the RAVE Act above.

    The common thread

    Our reading of the record: across Indiana and Astroworld, nobody with clear authority stopped the event in time. Indiana's reviews say so explicitly; in Houston, roughly half an hour passed between the mass-casualty declaration and the end of the show364361. Every production document in this guide can be perfect, and it still comes down to someone being empowered, and willing, to call it.

        21Open questions and known gaps

        What we could not pin down, so you know where the edges of this guide are:

        • The Wall of Sound's numbers. Driver counts run from about 586 to 650, the published wattage does not match the published amplifier count, and "first line array" rests on a 2001 article we could not read.
        • Crowd and attendance figures. Jarre's records are self-reported or Guinness figures nobody has independently checked; festival numbers are promoter-reported and count attendance across days.
        • Incident tolls. Tolls for recent fires moved after first reports (Kočani from 59 to 63, Crans-Montana from 40 to 41) and may move again.

        • Corporate deals. inMusic is integrating Native Instruments, but we found no formal closing announcement; nor any statement that the CDJ-3000 is discontinued, as of October 2026.
        • Standards and platform terms. Our 2026-edition details for NFPA 1126 and 160 come from a publisher's summary, not the standards themselves; Twitch DJ Program terms are as announced in 2024; Sphere's residency list comes from Wikipedia rather than Sphere.
        • CDJ-3000 and 3000X interoperability on the same PRO DJ LINK network: no primary source.
        • Audience-scanning exposure limits. We deliberately give no single number, because the real limit depends on wavelength and exposure time and the standards that define it were not available to us.

        22Quick answers

        Why is it called a gobo?

        Nobody knows. Merriam-Webster gives the origin as unknown, with a first known use in 1923 for a dark panel that blocked light from a film camera; "goes before optics" and similar acronyms are later backronyms, and "go-between" is only a possible guess.

        Why do CDJs need an Ethernet cable?

        PRO DJ LINK, introduced with the CDJ-2000 in 2009, connects players, mixer and rekordbox over Ethernet so one USB stick can feed every deck and the players can share beat and tempo data for sync and quantize. It does not carry audio, and more than two players need a network switch.

        Do CDJs still play CDs?

        Not the current ones. The CDJ-3000 and CDJ-3000X have no disc drive and play from USB, the network, the cloud and streaming services; "CDJ" is now a legacy name.

        What is a DMX universe?

        One DMX512 cable's worth of lighting control: 512 channels, each a value from 0 to 255, sent one way down a daisy chain of up to 32 devices and refreshed about 44 times a second. Big rigs need many universes, so they carry them over Ethernet with Art-Net or sACN.

        What is the difference between haze and fog?

        Fog is a dense, visible cloud. Haze is a fine, near-invisible suspension whose job is to make light beams and laser lines show up in the air; it can also set off photoelectric smoke detectors, so it is agreed with the venue in advance.

        Do you need a licence for a laser show in the US?

        Yes, above the Class IIIa limit of 5 mW visible, which covers almost every show laser. You need an FDA variance before the first show, audience scanning needs specific approval on top, and outdoor shows need an FAA review filed about 90 days ahead.

        What pixel pitch do I need for an LED wall?

        Pixel pitch is the distance in millimetres between pixel centres. Planar's rule of thumb is pitch times 10 equals comfortable viewing distance in feet, so a 3 mm wall looks good from about 30 feet, but manufacturers' formulas disagree widely.

        Why do LED screens look stripy on my phone?

        LEDs are dimmed by pulsing them very fast, and a phone's rolling shutter catches different numbers of pulses on different rows of its sensor, which shows up as dark bands. Higher refresh rates and genlock reduce it.

        What was the Grateful Dead's Wall of Sound?

        A 1974 PA of about 75 tons stacked behind the band in six separate systems, serving as both the house PA and the monitors. It toured from March to October 1974 and was retired because the costs spiralled.

        What does a production manager do?

        The production manager owns the technical side of a show: the technical advance, vendors, crew calls and the production schedule. The tour manager, by contrast, owns the tour's money, schedule and people, including settlement with the promoter.

        What goes in a DJ's technical rider?

        The production requirements: PA, monitors, DJ booth gear and layout, lighting, power and stage size, usually with exact player and mixer models. The stage plot and input list go with it and should be dated PDFs with a contact on every page.

        Are cold spark machines safe because they are cold?

        No. They heat titanium-zirconium powder to around 560 C, and fire officials treat them as pyrotechnics that need permits, certified operators and clearance from anything combustible.

        How loud is too loud at a club or festival?

        The World Health Organization recommends no more than 100 dB LAeq averaged over 15 minutes. Country limits vary, and the US has no federal audience limit; OSHA's 90 dBA limit covers workers only.

        Why shouldn't a DJ redline the mixer?

        The venue's limiter caps the maximum level, so pushing the mixer into the red does not make the room louder. It only flattens and distorts the music, so keep headroom at every gain stage.
